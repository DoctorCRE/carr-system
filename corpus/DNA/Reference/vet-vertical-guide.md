## Universal veterinary facts (apply across the vet verticals)

- **Degree:** vet school ≈ **4 years post-graduate** → **DVM = VMD** (same degree, different abbreviation).
- **Sizing:** small-animal and emergency clinics run **1,500–2,500 SF**; large-animal is an **acreage** play (1–3 acres); multi-specialty is **10,000–40,000 SF**.
- **The standard small-animal room list (the template layout):**
  - **Exam rooms** — 2–3 for an average office, each with **two access points** (one from the waiting room, one from the treatment area). Exam rooms open **directly off the waiting room**.
  - **Open treatment area** in back — treatment table, wash area, sterilization/prep.
  - **Surgery suite.**
  - **Kennel area** — **separate rooms for feline vs. canine.**
  - **Isolation room** for contagious animals.
  - **X-ray area** — may require **lead-lined walls** depending on proximity/local code.
  - **Lab area**, **supply + medication storage.**
  - **Comfort / Euthanasia room** — couch, dim, quiet, private.
  - **Staff lounge / break area.**
  - **Retail space in reception** — food (prescription + standard), toys, leashes, etc.
  - **Built-in scale near the front desk** (weigh pets at check-in).
  - **Grooming** (not in all clinics).
  - **Back or side door + a freezer near that door** to hold euthanized animals until removal (once or twice a week). This is a real due-diligence item — a vet needs discreet rear/side access.
- **Intake tool:** every page points to the **Vet Questionnaire** to drive the space conversation.
- **Why vet build-outs are expensive (and why the TI ask is large):** **smell and sound mitigation.** Vets are the *most* sensitive parties to both (see the pitch) — it's engineered into the build-out, which is what runs the cost up.

## The verticals at a glance

| Vertical | Size | Setting | Distinctives to flag |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Small Animal** (dogs/cats, sometimes other small pets) | 1,500–2,500 SF | Retail / center / MOB-style | The flagship layout above; dual-access exam rooms; separate cat/canine kennels; comfort/euthanasia room; back-door freezer |
| **Large Animal** (cows, horses, pigs, goats) | **1–3 acres of land** (rural) | Rural, land-based | Small reception + office + break room; **barns, stalls, corrals**; treatment often **in the stalls/barn**; surgery/procedure space with **doors sized for large animals** and **floor drains** |

## MEP specs — HVAC & electrical (merged 2026-07-31 from a sourced research pass; every LOI term is negotiable — these are the defensible most-common asks, not policy)

- **HVAC capacity:** no vet-specific published tons/SF exists; **≈1 ton per 250–300 SF** is the reasoned planning band (denser than the 350–400 office baseline) because kennel exhaust demands continuously conditioned makeup air and surgery wants filtered supply. Flag as rule-of-thumb if a landlord's engineer pushes back.
- **Air changes (the real spec, trade-guideline):** kennels/animal areas **8–12 ACH**; surgery **10–15 ACH filtered**; **isolation 12+ ACH, negative pressure, 100% exhaust — never recirculated**. Pressure zoning: reception positive, clinical neutral, kennels/wet/isolation negative. (dvm360 kennel-design guidance + AAHA environmental recommendations; re-verify the live AAHA page before quoting in a contract.)
- **Electrical:** **200A single-phase** covers a general practice. Digital x-ray wants a **dedicated single-phase 208V, 100–125A** line with disconnect (30–40kW generator class; momentary spikes 100–200A are why the line is dedicated — Maven Imaging / JPI Animal Health). **A fixed CT is the one true 3-phase trigger: 480V, ~150 kVA transformer, $7–10K install, possible utility coordination** — never fold it into a general electrical ask; it's its own negotiated line item. Cone-beam CT alternative runs on 220V single-phase; portable CT charges off a wall outlet (Antech).
- **Negotiation fact worth money: NEC Article 517 does NOT apply to veterinary** — its scope is human healthcare only (IAEI on 517.1). Hospital-grade receptacles/isolated grounds in a vet suite are best practice, not code; don't let a landlord's engineer price 517-grade wiring into the TI as mandatory.
- **LOI row shape (~2,500–3,500 SF GP with x-ray, no CT):** HVAC no leaner than ~300 SF/ton with independently ducted negative-pressure kennel/isolation exhaust (8–12 ACH minimum) separate from general return air; 200A single-phase service with a dedicated 208V/100–125A radiography circuit.
- **Gap flagged, not guessed:** cage/kennel dryer and O2-concentrator electrical specs didn't resolve to manufacturer sheets in the research pass — verify with the equipment vendor before writing a specific ampacity into a binding clause.

## Pitch to Landlord — veterinary (INTERNAL, negotiation — CARR proprietary, from the portal)

> **Internal negotiation content — not client-facing.** Why a landlord should *want* a vet tenant; used to win TI and free rent. Captured from the portal's own Vet Pitch page.

- **A veterinary use is THE most secure use a landlord can have.** **< 1% default rate even for startups**, and a built-out vet space in a nice center re-leases fast — a new lease is often executed **before the prior tenant is even out.** Vets rarely relocate (construction cost).
- **Justifying a large TI:** the TI protects the client's practice-loan position and funds the equipment the intended services require. Money spent building out the space is money that can't go toward being successful.
- **Downside is still fine for the landlord:** if the client relocates — or in the < 1% default case — the landlord still holds a very desirable, built-out asset. Contrast the bank: **the big national banks trip over themselves to write ~$500k startup loans**, yet on default the bank's only fallback is equipment worth **pennies on the dollar** (well over half the loan went to leasehold improvements + working capital). They hold billions of these loans anyway and compete hard for them.
- **Why vets don't default:** rather than bankruptcy, a struggling vet **sells the "bones" of the practice to a veterinary group** — there are **billions in enterprise money** pouring into vet acquisition. The landlord ends up with a *stronger* tenant, not an empty box.
- **Net-worth objection:** a young doctor often has *negative* net worth — but it's overwhelmingly **student-loan** debt, and banks still compete hard to lend (no collateral recourse) despite it.
- **"I don't like the vet use" — the deal-killer objection, two flavors (unique to vet, handle head-on):**
  1. **Smell.** Landlords fear odor permeating the center. Reality: **vets are the MOST sensitive party** — they don't want smells leaving their space, or even noticed by owners walking in, and they mitigate all the way back to the clinical area and kennels, because **pets are smell-sensitive, get nervous, and the vet loses clients.**
  2. **Sound.** Landlords fear barking noise. Reality: vets are **equally sensitive** — they mitigate sound both from leaving the space *and* from reverberating within it, because **echoing barks spike patient anxiety.**
  - Both mitigations are exactly why vet build-outs get expensive — which loops back to **why the big TI is justified.**

## Creditworthiness — external corroboration of the pitch (NOT CARR portal; cited)

*The vet pitch leans on "billions in enterprise money" in the acquisition market. That's real and quantifiable — use these if a landlord wants proof, with attribution:*
- **~$51.6 billion** of private-equity capital has gone into the veterinary sector through 2024 (**$9.3 billion in just the first four months of 2024**); as of 2021, **~25% of general practices and ~75% of specialty practices were corporately owned**, with consolidators controlling nearly half the market. Big platforms: **NVA (1,400+ hospitals, JAB), Thrive (380 clinics, TSG), Mission + Southern Vet Partners (730, Shore Capital).** Consolidators routinely pay **2–10× value** for community clinics. Source: AAHA Trends, "Corporate Consolidation and the Rise of Private Equity" (Brakke Consulting + PitchBook data). https://www.aaha.org/trends-magazine/publications/corporate-consolidation-and-the-rise-of-private-equity/
- This corroborates the pitch's core "a struggling vet sells to a group rather than defaulting" argument — the buyers, the dollars, and the premiums are documented.
