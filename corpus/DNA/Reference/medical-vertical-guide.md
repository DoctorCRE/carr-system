## Universal medical facts (apply to General Practice + most specialties)

- **Sizing rule of thumb:** **1,500 SF for the first doctor + 500–1,000 SF for each additional doctor.** Single-practitioner primary-care-type offices land **1,800–2,400 SF**; procedural and group specialties run larger (see table). Runs smaller in very expensive metros (SF, NYC).
- **Exam rooms (primary-care types):** **5–8 exam rooms, a sink in every exam room.**
- **Lab + samples:** if the practice runs an in-house lab and takes **urine** (IM, Family, OBGYN, Urology, Urgent Care) or **stool** (GI) samples, provide at least one **bathroom not accessed via the lobby** (ideally sharing a wall with the lab).
- **MEP (same spec as dental):**
  - **HVAC:** ≈ **1 ton per 250–350 SF** (more windows = more heat = more tonnage).
  - **Electrical:** **3-phase, 200-amp** sufficient up to 3,000 SF; ask **400-amp over 3,000 SF**. If only single-phase is delivered, consider adding amperage. In-house lasers / imaging / x-ray add load — ask early. *(Equipment detail, merged 2026-07-31 from a sourced research pass: office x-ray/DR = dedicated **single-phase 208V, 100–125A** with disconnect — 3-phase only enters above ~40kW generator output, hospital-radiology territory. A single dermatology/aesthetic laser is a **dedicated single-phase 220V/20–30A circuit** (Cynosure FDA filing); the 3-phase trigger is **3+ lasers/chillers running simultaneously** — aggregate load, not any one device. The one unambiguous 3-phase case is a **fixed CT: 480V, ~150 kVA transformer, its own negotiated line item**, never folded into a general ask.)*
  - **Parking:** ≈ **5 spaces per 1,000 SF** ideal (go higher on heavy shared-parking centers). Flag anything less.
- **Special build-out triggers to ask about:** in-house imaging (x-ray → **lead-lined** rooms; large equipment → extra power), lasers (extra electrical + code — **laser rooms also want their own HVAC zone holding 64–72°F**: laser duty cycles spike heat loads the suite system won't track, and platforms derate/fault outside their temperature band, per the IJDVL laser-room standard), **negative-pressure rooms / medical-gas lines** (procedural specialties), and refrigeration/ventilation for stored biologics or chemicals.
- **Intake tool:** every page points to the **Medical Questionnaire** to drive the space conversation (the medical analog to the dental questionnaire in `DNA/Clients/intake/`).

### The referral engine → site-selection logic (the single biggest cross-cutting pattern)
Nearly every **specialist** gets most business as **referrals from Primary Care / Family Practice** — so they want to sit in a **Medical Office Building (MOB)** near referral sources and **do not prioritize street visibility.** The exceptions are the front-door, walk-in, or cosmetic practices: **Family Medicine, Pediatrics, and Urgent Care are the flexible ones** (retail / office / stand-alone / MOB all work), and **cosmetic-leaning Plastic Surgery / Dermatology** actively *want* visibility and high-end finishes. Use this to match: a referral-driven specialist near a strong PCP cluster/MOB; a walk-in or cosmetic practice where visibility and access matter.

## The verticals at a glance

| Vertical | SF (single practitioner) | Space-type preference | Referral-driven? | Distinctives to flag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **General Practice** (general-medical) | 1,800–2,400 | Very flexible (retail/office/stand-alone/MOB) | Front door | 5–8 exam rooms, sink each; in-house lab + urine → extra bathroom off lobby |
| **Internal Medicine** | 1,800–2,400 | Often MOB (collaborate on complex/multi-system cases) | Front door + referrals | Complex/seriously-ill patients; ask about in-house imaging (x-ray → lead-lined) and lasers (extra electric) |
| **Family Medicine** | 1,800–2,400 | **Most flexible** of all (retail/office/stand-alone/MOB) | Front door | All ages; in-house lab + urine → extra bathroom off lobby |
| **Pediatrics** | 2,200–2,800 | Flexible (retail/office/stand-alone/MOB) | Front door | Sometimes **two waiting rooms (sick vs. well)**; larger waiting + play corners, more bodies per patient; **extra parking caution** (avoid busy shared lots); may add mental-health counseling space; **vaccine storage up to ~$75k** → refrigeration w/ alarms + backup generator |
| **Urgent Care** | 1,800–2,400 (chains/hospital-affiliated larger) | Flexible (retail/stand-alone; MOB if chain/hospital) | Walk-in | **X-ray in most centers**; in-house lab + urine → extra bathroom; 5–8 exam rooms; **downloadable floorplans on the portal** |
| **Orthopedics** | **3,000+** (often larger groups) | MOB or stand-alone | Referrals | Imaging space; exam-room space/lighting to read x-rays/MRIs; **casting room**; orthotics storage; PT and hospital/surgery-center proximity |
| **Plastic Surgeon** | 2,800–5,000 | MOB or stand-alone (sometimes retail); cosmetic → **visibility** | Mixed | High-end finishes; procedure rooms may need **negative-pressure rooms, gas lines, lasers** (special code); **separate entrance/exit** for post-procedure privacy |
| **Dermatology** | 2,500–5,000 | MOB or stand-alone (sometimes retail); cosmetic → visibility, medical → referral proximity | Mixed | Exam + treatment rooms; lasers/light-based → electrical; **MOHS** (skin cancer) → lasers + lab + **separate small waiting area between scraping rounds**; watch med-spa competition |
| **OBGYN** | 2,500–5,000 | Usually MOB; not street visibility | Referrals (PCP/FP) | Ultrasound/imaging; urine tests → bathroom off lobby; late-pregnancy **mobility/parking**; child entertainment + **extra exam-room space for spouses**; anatomy scans → special lighting/radiology |
| **ENT** | 2,800–5,000 | Usually MOB | Referrals | Procedure rooms; hospital/surgery-center proximity (tonsils, cochlear, ear tubes); **sophisticated audiology equipment** = expensive precise setup → relocation hesitancy |
| **Cardiology** | 2,500–5,000 | MOB; hospital proximity (surgery recoveries) | Referrals | EKG; **stress-test rooms** (treadmills + monitoring); ask about **nuclear medicine** (radioactive dyes → building considerations) |
| **Gastroenterology** | 2,500–5,000 | Usually MOB | Referrals (incl. UC/ED) | **Stool tests** → bathroom(s) not solely lobby-accessed; longer visits → larger waiting |
| **Pain Management** | 1,500–2,500 | Usually MOB | Referrals | Two models: **procedure-focused** vs. **interdisciplinary** (massage/PT/chiro/acupuncture); ask about in-house imaging devices |
| **Urology** | **3,000+** (group practices) | Usually MOB | Referrals | Ultrasound; urine tests → bathroom off lobby; hospital/surgery-center proximity for surgery-heavy practices |
| **Neurology** | **3,000–10,000** (larger groups) | Usually MOB | Referrals | Imaging area; exam + treatment rooms |
| **Nephrology** | 2,500–5,000 | Usually MOB | Referrals | Subspecialty of internal medicine (kidney); imaging area |
| **Rheumatology** | 2,500–5,000 | Usually MOB | Referrals | Immune/musculoskeletal; imaging equipment |
| **Endocrinology** | 2,500–5,000 | Usually MOB | Referrals | Hormones — thyroid/adrenal/pancreatic; exam + treatment rooms |
| **Radiology** | 2,500–5,000 | MOB advantageous (serves other practices) | Referrals (diagnostic assist) | **Large equipment draws heavy power**; specialized lighting or **lead-lined walls** |
| **Oncology** | **10,000** | MOB + hospital proximity | Referrals | Treatment/recovery + **chemical mixing room** (special ventilation/storage/disposal); larger parking + waiting (family); calm atmosphere; **hours-long infusions**, chemo nausea; on-site imaging q |
| **Surgery Center (ASC)** | **30,000–40,000** | Stand-alone or attached to a medical complex | Physician-owned | A **mini-hospital**: multiple check-ins, ORs, recovery (not overnight), imaging, large parking. Value prop: lower cost, easier scheduling, faster return home |

## Cross-cutting due-diligence checklist (pull from the table above, by trigger)
- **Referral-driven → MOB, low street-visibility priority:** IM, Ortho, Plastics(medical), Derm(medical), OBGYN, ENT, Cardiology, GI, Pain, Urology, Neuro, Nephro, Rheum, Endo, Radiology, Oncology.
- **Flexible / visibility-tolerant (retail OK):** Family Medicine, Pediatrics, Urgent Care; **cosmetic** Plastics/Derm actively want visibility.
- **Bathroom not off the lobby (samples):** urine — IM, Family, OBGYN, Urology, Urgent Care; stool — GI.
- **Extra power / lead-lining / lasers:** Radiology, IM & Urgent Care (x-ray), Cardiology (nuclear medicine), Derm & Plastics (lasers), OBGYN (anatomy scans).
- **Negative-pressure / medical-gas / special ventilation:** Plastics (procedure rooms), Oncology (chemical mixing room).
- **Hospital / surgery-center proximity:** Ortho, ENT, Cardiology, Urology, Oncology (+ any surgery-heavy practice).
- **Big-footprint outliers (don't size with the 1,500+500 rule):** Surgery Center 30,000–40,000; Oncology 10,000; Neurology up to 10,000; Ortho / Urology 3,000+.
- **Refrigeration + backup power:** Pediatrics (vaccine inventory up to ~$75k).

## Tenant security & creditworthiness — medical (external research, NOT from the CARR portal)

*Added July 7, 2026 to fill the gap the portal's medical pages leave open. Everything here is from public third-party sources (cited inline), NOT CARR proprietary training. Read the metric caveat before quoting a number — the "medical default rate" depends heavily on what's being measured.*

**Physician practices are among the lowest-default small businesses banks lend to — but the headline number depends on the metric.**
- On **lifetime (cumulative) charge-offs**, an analysis of ~40,000 SBA 7(a) loans (2008–2012 cohort tracked to June 30, 2023, SBA data compiled by CapTec) puts **Offices of Physicians at ~4.75%** and **Offices of Dentists at ~3.76%**, vs. a **~7.5% all-industry average** — i.e., medical and dental sit among the *lowest-default* cohorts (physicians ranked #20, dentists #10 of the top 100 industries). Source: sba7a.loans, "Industries With the Lowest Default Rates for SBA Loans." https://www.sba7a.loans/sba-7a-loans-small-business-blog/industries-with-the-lowest-default-rates-for-sba-loans/
- On an **annual / established-practice basis**, healthcare SBA borrowers run **below ~2%, often under 1% for established practices**, vs. a 2–3% overall SBA portfolio rate. Source: Crestmont Capital, "SBA Loan Default Rates by Industry." https://www.crestmontcapital.com/blog/sba-loan-default-rates-by-industry
- **Metric caveat (say this out loud before quoting a figure):** the CARR dental page's "<1% / <0.75%" and the SBA "~3.76% dentist" number are **not contradictory** — the CARR figure reads as an *annual* default on *conventional* practice lending to established doctors; the SBA number is a *cumulative lifetime loss* on *SBA-guaranteed* loans, which skew toward startups and higher-risk borrowers. Same conclusion either way (medical/dental are low-default), just different denominators. Don't blend the two into one stat.

**Medical office real estate itself is exceptionally stable — the sector-level version of the same argument.**
- **Record-high occupancy 92.7%** (March 2026), absorption consistently outpacing new deliveries, and MOBs have outperformed the broader office sector every year since 2022; health systems were **46% of medical leasing** tracked in 2025 (creditworthy anchor tenants). Source: JLL, 2026 Medical Outpatient Building Perspective. https://www.jll.com/en-us/insights/medical-outpatient-building-perspective
- Vacancy sits in **single digits**, and medical tenants "**historically stay longer than their lease terms**" because they invest heavily in build-out and "pay their bills." Source: Paul Zeman, Bull Realty, "Why Medical Office Buildings Remain a Safe Bet." https://www.bullrealty.com/intel/blog-posts/view/why-medical-office-buildings-remain-a-safe-bet-in-commercial-real-estate

**The "sells rather than defaults" dynamic exists in medicine too — the analog to dental DSOs is hospital/health-system, PPM, and PE consolidation.** A struggling practice is far more often an *acquisition target* than an empty box:
- **47% of physicians** were employed by or affiliated with **hospital systems** in 2024 (up from **<30% in 2012**); **6.5%** were in **private-equity-owned** practices in 2024 (up from 4.5% in 2022); ~2% insurer-owned. Source: U.S. GAO, Health Care Consolidation report, Sept 24, 2025 (citing AMA data). https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/physician-practice-acquistions-healthcare-consolidation-rising-government-accountability-office-gao/760919/

## Pitch to Landlord — medical (INTERNAL, negotiation — built from the research above, not the CARR portal)

> **Internal negotiation content — not client-facing.** The portal did not supply this for the medical branch; assembled here from the cited third-party data so you have the same TI/free-rent argument you have on the dental side. Because it rests on external sources (not CARR's proprietary numbers), attribute or soften the stats if you put them in front of a landlord.

- **A physician tenant is one of the most secure uses a landlord can hold.** Medical/dental practices are among the lowest-default SBA borrowers (well below the ~7.5% all-industry lifetime average; often under 1% annually for established practices), and MOB space runs record-high occupancy (~92.7%) with single-digit vacancy while tenants stay *past* their lease terms.
- **Justifying a large TI:** physicians sink heavy capital into build-out (imaging, lab, procedure rooms, lead-lining, gas) — that investment is exactly why they're sticky and why they pay their bills. A bigger TI protects the practice's capital for equipment and keeps a long, low-turnover tenant in place.
- **Downside is still fine for the landlord:** a built-out medical suite in a good MOB re-leases into a sector where absorption outpaces deliveries. And a struggling practice rarely goes dark — it gets **acquired** by a hospital system, PPM, or PE platform (47% of physicians are already hospital-affiliated, PE ownership rising), so the landlord tends to end up with a *stronger, better-capitalized* tenant on the same lease, not a vacancy.
- **Same firewall as dental:** this Pitch is internal negotiation logic — not a social post, not client-facing.
