## Universal dental facts (apply to GP + all specialties)

- **Degree:** DDS = DMD (same degree; which one depends on the school). Dental school ≈ 4 years after a bachelor's. Specialties add a 2–3 year residency.
- **Sizing formula:** **usable SF ≈ (# of operatories) × 400 SF.** E.g. 5 operatories → 2,000 usable SF. Runs smaller in very expensive metros (SF, NYC). Single-practitioner offices are typically **1,500–2,500 SF** depending on specialty.
- **Operatory size:** ≈ 10.5' wide × 12' long. (Oral-surgery *surgery* rooms are bigger, ≈ 12' × 14'.)
- **Creditworthiness (key negotiation fact):** dentists of ALL specialties default **< 0.75%** (> 99.25% success); even startups < 1%. Very solid long-term tenants. Banks (BofA, Wells) compete to lend ~$500k startup loans with essentially no collateral.
- **Hygiene drives revenue:** the more hygiene patients flow through, the more restorative work gets done — so the hygiene area materially affects a practice's financial success. (Ortho and endo don't do hygiene; see below.)
- **Chairside delivery:** "Side / Rear / Over-the-Patient delivery" = handpieces, water, and vacuum come from the side wall, the head wall / cabinet, or are chair-mounted.

### MEP & site (standard healthcare due-diligence items for dental)
- **Plumbing:** sinks in or near each operatory; **vacuum, compressed air, water** (plumbed, or a self-contained in-operatory system). Optional oxygen / nitrous (can also be rolling canisters).
- **HVAC:** ≈ **1 ton per 250–350 SF** (more windows = more heat = more tonnage). Zone it — a 2,500 SF 5-op practice commonly runs **3 zones** (clinical / sterilization / business office); **sterilization wants its own exhaust** for autoclave steam/heat.
- **Electrical:** **3-phase, 200-amp** panel is sufficient up to 3,000 SF; ask for **400-amp over 3,000 SF**. If only single-phase is delivered, consider adding amperage. *(Equipment detail, merged 2026-07-31 from a sourced research pass: 3-phase is an efficiency choice at this scale, not a hard requirement — RAMVAC's 3 HP dry vacuum ships in both 230V single-phase (16A) and 3-phase (8.4A) versions, compressors >2 HP want 240V/30A dedicated, autoclaves are 115V/15A dedicated (Midmark M9/M11), and **dental CBCT runs on a dedicated 220–240V 20–30A SINGLE-phase circuit — it is not a medical CT and never justifies a 3-phase demand**, a misconception worth correcting when a landlord's engineer over-scopes. Circuit count is the real load story: 2–3 dedicated 20A circuits PER operatory, so a 4-op practice needs 12–15 dedicated circuits before lighting and HVAC. Operatories/sterilization/imaging are NEC Article 517 patient-care spaces: metal raceway + insulated ground, code not preference.)*
- **Parking:** **≈ 5 spaces per 1,000 SF** is ideal (go higher if the center's shared parking load is heavy). Flag anything less to the client.
- **Code triggers:** rooms containing **medical gases** may require ventilation directly outside (**nitrous scavenging: 40–45 L/min per workstation, vented outdoors away from any fresh-air intake** — OSHA/ADA guidance, treat the vent location as non-negotiable); **sedation dentistry** likely requires the building to be **sprinklered**.

### Equipment glossary
- **Pan (panograph):** ear-to-ear x-ray of the mouth (2D or 3D imaging).
- **PA (periapical):** x-ray highlighting one or two teeth.
- **Ceph arm:** added to a pano for fuller imaging (common for ortho → "Pan/Ceph").
- **Crown milling (E4D / CEREC):** in-office machines that mill ceramic crowns (GP).
- **3D imaging** and (endo) **operatory microscopes**.

| **General Dentist (GP)** | 1,800–2,400 | 4–5 ops (1–2 hygiene + 2–3 treatment); full room list + private checkout | Yes | Refers complex cases out | Hygiene + restorative; crown milling; broadest scope |

| **Prosthodontist** (aesthetic restoration / full-mouth) | 1,800–2,400 | 4–5 ops + **larger lab** (denture fabrication) | Sometimes | Heavily (unless takes hygiene) | +2–3yr; "most skilled"; lower caseload, larger cases |

## Pitch to Landlord — dental (INTERNAL, negotiation)

> **Internal negotiation content — not client-facing.** Why a landlord should *want* a dental tenant, used to win TI and free rent.

- **Most secure use a landlord can have.** < 1% default even for startups, and a built-out dental space in a nice center re-leases fast — often a new lease is signed *before the prior tenant is even out*. Dentists also rarely relocate (construction cost).
- **Justifying a large TI:** a bigger TI protects the practice's loan position and funds the equipment needed to perform the intended dentistry; money the client spends on the space is money that can't go toward being successful.
- **Downside is still fine for the landlord:** if the client relocates — or, in the < 1% case, defaults — the landlord still holds a very desirable, built-out asset. Contrast that with the bank: well over half the loan went to leasehold improvements + working capital, so on default the bank's only fallback is equipment worth pennies on the dollar — yet BofA / Wells compete hard to write these ~$500k startup loans anyway.
- **Why dentists don't default:** rather than bankruptcy, a struggling dentist sells the built-out practice + patient base to a dental group (billions in DSO/enterprise money chasing acquisitions) — so the landlord ends up with a *stronger* tenant, not an empty box.
- **Net-worth objection:** a young doctor often has *negative* net worth (mostly student loans). Meet it head-on: it's overwhelmingly student debt, and banks still compete hard for these loans — with no collateral recourse — despite it.

## Dental groups as buyers — what a group actually looks for (added 2026-07-29)

*Source: a Southeast multi-practice dental group we represent (C-156), president-level, in conversation. Useful two ways: qualifying a group client's search, and telling a solo owner what the group buying their practice will care about.*

**Acquisition buy box (a working example, not a universal standard).** A ~19-practice group's minimum on an established practice: **at least 6 operatories** and **at least $750,000 in annual revenue**. Below either line it isn't worth the integration cost to them. Real estate is deliberately not the deciding factor — a group at this size will buy the building or lease it, whichever closes the practice deal, and will not let the real estate structure kill an acquisition it wants.

**What that means for us.** On the group side, the 6-op floor sets the minimum viable building: **6 ops ≈ 2,400 usable SF** by the sizing formula above, so anything under roughly 2,400 SF is off their board regardless of price. On the solo-owner side, this is the concrete answer to "would a group buy my practice?" and it's a useful, non-salesy thing to be able to tell a doctor thinking about their exit.

**Why the group model creates recurring real estate demand.** Groups typically seat **one equity-holding dentist per location**. Associates who are two to three years out start wanting ownership, and they leave to get it — not because they're unhappy, but because the ambitious ones want equity in something. So a group with a bench of young associates has to keep opening locations, in the places those associates want to live, just to retain them. That makes their expansion demand structural and recurring rather than opportunistic. *(Prospecting implications in `DNA/Leads/pipeline-craft.md` §2.)*

**The bank-discipline mechanism behind the low default rate.** The startup default numbers above (<1%) are not luck, and a group operator's explanation is worth borrowing for the landlord pitch: the handful of major banks that write dental startup loans **underwrite the deal to cash flow** and hold the doctor inside it. They cap total project cost so the doctor can't get overextended, they know what rent the practice can actually carry, and they know what a given doctor is capable of producing. The bank sets the guardrails, which is why the loans perform. Same operator put the national figure on startup-lender defaults at **under 2%**, consistent with CARR's own <1% on dentists generally.

**Landlord-pitch use:** this upgrades "dentists rarely default" from a statistic to a mechanism. A landlord who hears *why* the number is low — a specialist lender has already stress-tested this tenant's rent against their production before they ever walked in — takes it more seriously than a bare percentage.

## Market cycle note — the consolidation hangover (added 2026-07-29)

*Same source, their read on why acquisition inventory is thin. Treat as an informed operator's thesis, not verified fact.*

Practices for sale are scarce right now because the roll-up cycle stalled. PE groups bought practices, combined them, then rates rose and they could not flip the combined platforms to the next buyer. So they are holding assets that are underperforming against the plan. The expected next phase: lenders pull back on weak locations, and since banks want to be in neither the dental business nor the real estate business, those locations get sold off — producing a wave of **resales of existing, already-built-out practices**.

Corroborating observation from our own market: larger groups have had locations sit **dark for months** before being re-tenanted.

**What to do with it:** watch for group-owned locations going dark or quietly listing. A built-out dental box coming back to market is a live opportunity on both sides — a buyer client who wants an existing practice, and a landlord who suddenly needs a qualified dental tenant. *(Sourcing angle in `DNA/Leads/pipeline-craft.md` §2.)*
