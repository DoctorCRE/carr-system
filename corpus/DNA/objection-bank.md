## The frame
An objection is not a rejection. Someone who objects read it, processed it, and cared enough to answer, that puts them ahead of 95% of your list. So the move is always the same three beats: **acknowledge → reframe toward one small next step → leave the door open.** No pressure, no false urgency. In a market this size, a pushy reputation costs more than a lost deal.

## The objection bank
*Reframes, not rebuttals. Keep your real voice; these are the bones.*

**"Our lease isn't up for a couple years."**
The best outcomes start 18 to 24 months out, because that's when you still have the leverage of time and the landlord knows it. A quick read now costs you nothing and tells you whether your renewal clock is being set up in your favor or theirs. Want me to map your timeline?

**"We're happy where we are, not looking to move."**
Most of my clients weren't moving either. The win is usually staying, on better terms. Worth fifteen minutes to see whether your renewal is being structured for you or for the landlord?

**"We already have a broker / a guy."**
Good, most of my clients came to me after their first deal. Happy to be a second set of eyes on one number, no obligation. If your guy's got it handled, you'll know in ten minutes.

**"Not a priority right now."**
Understood. When does your lease or renewal clock actually hit? I'll circle back at the moment it matters instead of bugging you before then, and I'll note the date so it doesn't slip.

**"Just send me some info."**
Happy to. What's most useful, a read of your renewal math or a quick market snapshot for your area? Or give me ten minutes and I'll show you on your actual numbers instead of a generic PDF.

**"What do you charge?"**
On tenant and buyer representation the landlord or seller side typically covers the fee, so it usually costs you nothing. Easiest to show how that maps to your specific situation in fifteen minutes.

**"We're not going to buy a building."**
Most practice owners shouldn't, at least not yet. The point isn't to sell you a building, it's to know which years buying actually beats leasing for you, so you don't leave money on the table either direction.

**"Uncertain times, not the market to make moves."**
Uncertainty is exactly when lease terms get mispriced. A quick look tells you whether to move, hold, or renegotiate, and that's useful whether you act now or not.

## Worked example: recovering representation after the client already toured and met the listing side

*Added 2026-07-29 from Joe and Dell's 7/28 call with a dental startup in Moultrie GA (L-208 / C-155). Kept because the setup was not ideal and the recovery worked.*

**The setup, and why it happened.** The referring vendor was brand new to CARR and had not been coached yet, so he sent the doctor straight to a building instead of sending the doctor to CARR. By the time CARR got the call the doctor had already toured the space, met the listing agents and the owner's son face to face, and verbally agreed a stair-step rent. Nothing was on paper, which is the only reason there was still a deal to represent.

**The standard this violates, and the fix.** Vendors send the doctor BEFORE any space is toured. That is a coaching conversation, not a rule you can enforce after the fact, and it belongs in the first lunch with every new referral partner. The cost of skipping it shows up exactly here.

**The four beats that recovered it.**

1. **Name the separation, do not attack the agent.** "Most landlords hire representation for separation." The doctor gets the concept as normal market practice rather than as a warning about the person he just shook hands with. No one has to be the villain.
2. **Explain transaction brokerage plainly.** The listing agent can work both sides, and they will lean toward the side that gave them the listing. Said once, without heat. The doctor got there himself: "they're working for them."
3. **Answer the fee question before it becomes an objection, then answer it again when asked.** The commission is already set aside in the listing agreement and gets split. He asked "how are y'all getting paid" anyway, which is the tell that it always needs saying twice, once unprompted and once on his terms.
4. **Convert the abstract into specific technical exposure he cannot check alone.** HVAC tonnage for the mechanical room, 400A service where the panel is probably 200A, an operating-expense cap on the NNN pass-throughs, who pays if the landlord does not. This is what actually closed it. Fiduciary duty is a concept; a 200A panel under a dental buildout is a number he can lose money on.

**Then the sequence, in order, and the order matters.** Representation agreement signed first. Then ask him to send the listing agent's contact rather than getting it around him. Then CARR contacts the listing agent and routes all questions through CARR from that point. Then LOI, terms on a spreadsheet, legal review, execution.

**What made it land.** He was already emotionally committed to the building and not shopping alternatives, so there was no leverage from competing options. The only leverage left was in the terms, and that is precisely what representation sells. Nothing was oversold, no alarm was raised about the space he loves.

## The outreach levers (use tastefully)
The psychology that works, filtered to what fits an honest local broker. Never manufacture, only surface what's true.

- **Reciprocity, made effortful.** Give something specific to THEM before any ask, a read of their actual lease clause, a market number for their block, an intro to a great lender. A generic "free tips" attachment triggers nothing; a specific insight about their situation does.
- **Curiosity gap (open loop).** Lead with a specific, incomplete result: "A practice a few miles from you changed one lease term and cut roughly $40k off a 10-year term." The open loop pulls the reply; a "free audit" creates no tension.
- **Cost of inaction, honestly.** The escalator "second mortgage" framing is loss aversion done right, name what a standard-looking clause quietly costs. Only ever when it's actually true for them.
- **Micro-yes before the ask.** Get a small agreement first: "Does this even apply to your situation?" Once they say yes, "worth fifteen minutes?" is consistent with what they just agreed to.
- **Assumptive but soft CTA.** "Want me to send the one-pager?" or "Worth a quick look?" beats "would you possibly be open to maybe chatting sometime?" Confidence reads as competence; hedging invites the no.
- **Follow-ups carry new information.** Most meetings come from follow-up, but never "just bumping this up," which only confirms their decision to ignore you. Every touch brings a new fact, angle, or proof point. Space them like a human who cares, not a machine.
- **Multi-channel familiarity.** A target who sees your name in a helpful reply, then a comment, then a note within a short window stops reading you as a stranger. Coordinate the channels toward the people you actually want, don't just broadcast.
