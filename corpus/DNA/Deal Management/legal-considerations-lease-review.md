## The 30-second lease skim — two tells before you read line by line

Ali's first-glance reads on a 50–60 page lease:
- **Security deposit** — asking 2+ months signals the landlord either doesn't understand the healthcare business model or doesn't trust it. It's a window into landlord psychology and foreshadows a harder negotiation ("this is a risky deal, we're putting a lot in...").
- **Assignment & subletting section** — where the **recapture provision** and other landmines hide. A recapture provision on a doctor's office is a deal-breaker: no one invests $500k+ to build out a space the landlord can claw back on a death, disability, or sale.

## Recapture / assignment — why healthcare cares so much

A healthcare practice will face accidents, disability, bringing on partners, selling to a group, consolidation — all real, all requiring the ability to assign. Against a $500k+ build-out, restrictive assignment or a recapture clause is financially untenable. **~8 of 10 times** the landlord can be *educated* into fixing it (CARR's consultative style + the attorney explaining why). The goal isn't to flip all the power to the tenant — it's to **even it out** so the landlord can't arbitrarily reject a qualified assignee. If a difficult landlord truly won't move on recapture AND has terrible assignment language, that's a rare "walk to another space" situation.

## Mom-and-pop vs. institutional landlords

- **Mom-and-pop:** easier legal negotiation, but weaker economics — less TI ($40–60/SF), less free rent (9 mo construction + 3 mo ramp is the strong version they *don't* get).
- **Institutional / sophisticated:** more complex leases (harder legal fight) but much stronger economic terms.
- Ali's preference: **the bigger landlords** — because the economics are better for the client, and the extra legal fight is worth it. Doctors face enough headwinds; the money/free rent matters.

## The top clauses in a healthcare lease

1. **Possession / commencement / rent-commencement dates** — construction runs long; get crystal clear on when possession starts and when rent starts, and coordinate with contractors.
2. **Rental obligation — especially operating expenses.** In triple-net / double-net leases OpEx can be ~a third of the rent or more; doctors fixate on base rent and miss it. **Junk fee to reject: a management fee charged on property tax** — you already pay your share of the tax; a management fee on top is "silly." Ali never lets clients agree to it.
3. **Repairs** — who's responsible for what.
4. **Assignment & subletting** (see above).
5. **Rules & regulations of the center/building** — easy to overlook: can a pedo practice hang TVs? Could the oral surgeon build a medical-surgical suite? Some centers prohibit exactly what the practice needs.
6. **Option to extend** — how many, and how is renewal rent calculated? After 10–15 years of 3–4% annual escalations you're usually well over market, so the option often *isn't* to the tenant's advantage — which is why Ali pushes clients in **year 7–8 of a 10-year lease** to start looking to buy a building (a major CARR national referral driver).

## Standard office vs. healthcare lease

Standard office is usually full-service, ~3-year, little TI. Healthcare carries a huge TI investment, so assignment and OpEx language make outsized financial difference. Doctors who fail usually didn't understand what they signed — triple-net vs. full-service, or the true OpEx magnitude. Working with a landlord who has **done healthcare deals starts you "at second base"** — they already know not to push percentage rent or quarterly-financials-on-the-business demands.

## The "punt it to the lease" tactic — how to keep a deal from dying at the LOI

If a single point (e.g. exclusivity or assignment) is the last sticking point in the LOI and threatens to kill the deal: **agree to everything else and punt that one point to the lease negotiation** rather than dying at the LOI. Ali calls this a "super sophisticated" move. Why it works:
- The client is exhausted (often 6 months–2 years of searching) and at risk of defecting to a practice purchase; don't lose the deal over one term.
- You get the deal moving, and you usually have **better leverage a month down the road** than at the standoff.
- Tennis analogy: "it's not about the perfect return — just get the ball back and start the point." If you never reach the lease, the game never started.
- **Guardrail:** never agree in the LOI to something you can't live with — the landlord will say "that was in the LOI, we're moving to lease." Punt = defer the *unresolved* point, don't concede it.

## The relationships close

Ali's parting advice: **build relationships with local developers, landlords, and brokers.** Relationships drive smooth deals more than aggression. CARR's early "go crush the landlord" energy matures into: negotiate aggressively for the client *and* invest in relationships, because that's what actually closes deals and gets terms agreed. (Ali's firm turns lease reviews in ~3 days on the strength of those relationships.)

## Reviewing the executed lease & work letter — whose job, and how to protect yourself
*(Added July 7, 2026 — from the founder Nov 2024 Q&A; source in the ingestion ledger.)*
- **Order of responsibility:** the **attorney** reads the full lease + work letter first (there should be an attorney on any deal with a work letter), then **CARR**, then the **client**. Agents **can't practice law** — you can't interpret substantive legal issues or "read the lease and advise." But you absolutely **should read the lease and work letter enough to confirm the business points you negotiated made it in.**
- **The agent's specific job:** compare the **LOI/RFP ↔ the final lease** and catch discrepancies — the rate, free-rent months, and especially the **TI/renovation allowance** (e.g. "$40/SF TI" when you negotiated "$55"). The **work letter is substantive** — it *is* the TI/renovation allowance you negotiated — so at minimum know where your deal points live and whether they're accurately reflected.
- **When something's off or unclear:** flag it to the listing broker ("looks like you missed this?" — usually a quick fix) and to the attorney **in writing**, and get the attorney's written sign-off. Put the confirmation request in writing: "here are the LOI terms — please confirm as attorney, with the client, that they're accurately represented in the lease."
- **Why it matters:** the attorney carries the highest legal liability, but they'll point at *you* as the real-estate expert "who should have known better" if a negotiated term didn't make the lease. A written attorney confirmation is your best protection against both the issue and the accusation. (You don't need to read an 80-page lease verbatim or memorize it — just reliably identify your business deal points and whether they're correct.)
