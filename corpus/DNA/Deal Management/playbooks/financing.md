# Part A — How banks underwrite
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from CARR's "Lending" EDU call (a senior CARR agent + an ex-banker CARR agent, ex-PNC/BofA banker) plus the session's own on-page write-up. Understanding this lets you **pre-qualify a deal** — don't show a doctor a $1M parcel they can't finance. Verbatim material and timestamps not retained (paraphrase-only policy, July 8, 2026); the source session is identified in the Source Material ingestion ledger. **Lender names/amounts/rates are ~2021 — directional; confirm current before quoting.***

> **Why CARR is on every deal:** dental loans are prized (low default), so national lenders compete hard. **Get 1–2 national dental lenders on every deal** to *set the terms/benchmark*, even if the doctor prefers a local bank — then the local bank can match or the doctor decides what the relationship is worth. If BofA "owns" a market and isn't treating you well, have an alternative (a deal was saved by an out-of-state banker with a different way of looking at it).

## The three loan types (know which one the client needs)
Most banks assume you want a **CRE loan** (real estate = hard collateral). But most dental clients need a **practice loan** — the *practice/production is the asset*, no real estate to lean on. **Watch for "hard-asset" banks:** they'll try to collateralize the equipment (only ~$110k on a $700k practice) and expect the doctor to cover the gap in cash → a no-go for acquisitions. Hard-asset banks are fine for *CRE* loans; set that expectation.

## Acquisitions — lending on the "charts" (Goodwill)
- Banks lend **80–90% of gross revenue** (last year, or the avg of the last 2–5) — banking term: **"enterprise value"** (intrinsic value from gross revenue + profitability; dental profits ~27–40%). It's really the **charts / patient base / blue-sky asset**: the new doctor keeps 90%+ of patients, so default is very low.
- It's a **predictability model**: national lenders ($5B books, thousands of deals) know ~90% of gross revenue tends to succeed; lend above that and deals go bad. It's *also* an asset — if it fails, the bank resells the practice to another doctor.
- **Off GROSS revenue, not collections/production** (each bank nuances). Most practices sell at **60–85% of collections** (banks may go to 100%, but rare).
- **Specialty goodwill doesn't transfer** — oral surgery/endo/perio are referral- and reputation-based (not location-based), so they transition far less than general practices (whose goodwill is fungible). Banks know this.

## Startups — go CONVENTIONAL, not SBA
- The lender pool shrinks; most banks default to **SBA**, but **dental startups get better terms CONVENTIONALLY** with national lenders — dozens do a **fully-amortized 10-yr loan (no balloon) at 100% financing.** If a bank says "SBA" for a startup, tell them they won't get startup intros (not competitive).
- **Projections-based lending**: reverse the math — to borrow $500k at ~77%, the doctor's CPA-prepared year-1 projection needs to show ~$580–650k gross (legitimizing all expenses, incl. rent).
- **Amounts (~2021, directional):** BofA — single GD **$500k**, specialty **$550k**, two doctors up to **$600k**; Huntington — **$550–600k** even for a single doctor; Wells — **no max**, based on historical earnings + liquidity (will do **$750k** if the doctor earned equivalent income as an associate/partner).
- **Concentration risk (counterintuitive):** banks lend *more* to two-doctor partnerships than a single doctor — if one is hurt/leaves, the other sustains the office.
- **Structure:** interest-only up to 1 yr, then partial-principal 6mo–1yr. Buckets ≈ **buildout $250k · equipment $175k · misc $50k · working capital $75k** (2–3 months payroll); money moves between buckets *except* working capital.
- **Max rent caps**: BofA ~**$6,000/mo** (but one got approved at $91k first-year rent — case-by-case, make the market/geography argument); Huntington varies by banker ($65k, no step-ups, 3% escalation in one case); Wells weighs year-one rent. **This is a big reason CARR is on the deal — we ensure enough TI AND that the rent lands within the bank's cap.**
- **The doctor associates 2+ days/week** at first → outside income comforts the bank.

## CRE / ground-up — 100% financing for established doctors
- **SBA has two types:** **7A** (general) and **504** (must include real estate; the usual ground-up vehicle). Not all lenders do 504s (exposure) — an "SBA lender" (e.g. Live Oak) doesn't necessarily do both.
- **100% financing via practice equity**: practice doing $1.5M → 90% = ~$1.35M equity; minus existing practice debt (~$300k) = ~$1M the doctor can contribute *instead of cash*. On a $3.7M deal needing 15% down (~$555k), the ~$1M practice equity covers it → 100% financing. (Real estate side usually wants 15% down; they'll "bend the bamboo" to 100% on the practice side.) *(On-page example: $700k practice, $150k debt → $550k net equity × 85% = $467,500 available for the RE down payment.)*
- **Two-part structure**: close on the **land first** (short due-diligence window), then build within ~2 yrs at a predictable rate. RE amortized over **20–25 years** (not 10) → a $3–4M project is ~$20k/mo, easy to cash-flow. The bank won't count other tenants' rent — the practice must cash-flow the mortgage alone.
- **Predictability before the appraisal:** need competitive contractor bids (~$1.8M for a 9,000 SF exterior). Client upfront costs: civil engineer ~$20k, architect elevation + basic floor plan ~$5k, full MEPs $3.50–5/SF — minimize upfront (~$25–30k) in case it's not approvable. If costs come in high, they own the dirt and can't get the loan → shrink the project (client's at risk).
- **Interior build-out doesn't appraise** → either lobby the appraiser (not always kosher) or line up a separate internal **practice TI loan** to cover the appraisal gap.
- **Always have a conventional backup** (e.g. SBA 100% at 5.5% vs. conventional 90% at 3.1% with 10% down) in case policies/deals change.

## The banker vs. the underwriter — the practical key
The banker you meet (a Phil DeMarco / Cody / Wendy Caruso) **sources** the deal but does **not** underwrite it — they hand it to an internal **underwriter** plus a **project/vendor account manager** (who works with the doctor). The named banker only reappears when there's a problem.
- **Banker talent varies** — green/rigid rule-followers won't push; know their **managers** and lobby the next level for a variance.
- **Peyton's technique (next-level):** spell out ALL the deal points *for the underwriter* — TI on rentable SF vs. construction on usable SF (load factor), the abatement/free-rent months, months 1–5 vs 6–12 rent, a year-1 and year-2 **blended average**, with the **LOI + lease attached.** Removes the ambiguity that makes an underwriter see "$90k year-one rent → decline." *Take control of the deal.*
- **Understand the underwriter**: they put their **name** on it; a bad deal shrinks their signature authority (humiliating). So give them **rational ammunition** to say yes (hot market, no competition, productive client). Goal: "when CARR's involved, deals go well."

## TI-reimbursement timing — a deal-killer to manage early
BofA required **two-step TI reimbursement** — asking the *landlord* to pay half the TI mid-construction, not at the end. There's a **circular lien problem**: the contractor must release liens before the landlord pays TI, but the bank's lien isn't perfected until contractor liens clear. A deal **blew up mid-construction** when BofA wouldn't fund the contractor until the landlord paid. Where the gap money comes from: (1) client floats the cash, (2) bank over-lends then gets repaid, (3) GC signs lien waivers early (risking it).
- **Local-bank workaround**: negotiate **no prepayment penalty** so when the doctor gets the ~$180k landlord reimbursement (day 30–60) they make a **principal reduction** (not a refi). **Tell the underwriter about this "liquidity event"** so they factor it in and approve a deal that otherwise wouldn't pencil.
- **Ask the landlord to pay TI in thirds** (a third mid-way, etc.) — you'll hear "no" more than "yes," but it saves the doctor interest, and the landlord's "what if they run away?" fear is weak (the doctor has huge skin in the game).

## Free rent can HURT the loan (counterintuitive)
Lenders weigh **year-one rent**. Six months free means months 7–12 are higher (e.g. $9,500/mo); averaged, that can look *worse* than $4,500/mo from day one. **Fix:** spread the free rent (take 3 of 6 months and distribute), or weigh the runway vs. the higher average. And **watch CAM/triple-nets** hiding in the rent number ($6k base + $500–600 CAM bites you). On first-gen/new developments you can sometimes get a **cap on CAM — but ask for a cap on *controllables*, not all triple-nets** (they can't control taxes/insurance).

# Part B — Execution on live deals
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from CARR's "Financing 201" 2026 National Meeting panel — a senior CARR agent moderating, **the rapid-build producer** (Manhattan; ~90% leasing) and **a producer panelist** (Denver; purchases & ground-up). Here: how to work a lender on a live deal, structure a lease to pass underwriting, time a purchase's due diligence by bank, and use seller financing. Spans dental/medical/vet + leasing/purchase/ground-up. **Rates/amounts are illustrative ~2026 market examples — directional.** Mostly INTERNAL agent craft; two doctor-facing content angles are broken out at the end.*

## Lender relations — the pitch and the targets
- **Pitch yourself as a problem-solver, not a deal-beggar** (Brad). Don't open with "how many deals you got? how many docs in your portfolio?" Open with *"Find me the deals that stalled — the ones you couldn't work out or had broker trouble on — and let me help you get them done."* You get paid when they close a deal they otherwise couldn't. (Extends the banker playbook's "make it about them.")
- **Pin down the bank's RENT constraint up front** (Evan). "We'll lend this doctor $950k" is not enough — ask *"are you putting a firm rent cap on the loan?"* In a high-rent market that cap is the whole ballgame (you can't build a dental office on a too-tight rent budget). Gauge where their underwriters sit *before* touring.
- **Go past the main 5–6 practice-finance groups** (Provide, BofA, Huntington, Wells). Two under-worked lanes:
  - **Business bankers** — every business banker has a few medical clients they want to add value to; that's your opening. (Evan: "they're my primary vendor and I still need more.")
  - **Specialty / regional banks for the big or multi-doc uses** the nationals won't touch (a 30,000 SF rheumatology group, urgent-care rollups on institutional money that don't care whether rent is $7k or $9k). BofA/Provide won't do a six-doc practice — those go to a relationship bank via a line of credit. In Birmingham there were banks "you'd never have heard of" doing these because they can get creative on guarantor structure (a national bank needs all 15 docs to 100% guarantee; regionals flex).
- **Know the other bank roles** (Mike): the practice-finance lender is one seat — there's also a separate **business banker** and a **wealth-management** person (who never touches the loan, just moves documents/money). 5–6 positions per bank most agents never think about.
- **The more sophisticated the client, the easier the deal** (Evan): big healthcare groups trust you, take more space (= more commission), and aren't rent-sensitive. Worth chasing (he's targeting JP Morgan's middle-market healthcare conference to reach who funds the urgent-cares).

## Working the underwriter (the person who actually approves)
The salesperson/lender you talk to is **not** the underwriter — the underwriter has final say. Big nationals = strict (government oversight, narrow products/amounts); smaller banks = more room. Building on `financing.md`'s banker-vs-underwriter basics, the 201 moves:
- **The lease is the key document — get it to underwriting ASAP and never let the rent change after it's out** (Evan). Tell every banker up front: *"If you tell me the rent has to be X, I'll make the document say X — I want this loan to happen."* Structure it to pass underwriting *and* satisfy the client. It's all legal; they just have a fixed way of scoring risk.
- **Spell everything out for the underwriter.** They misread deals — e.g. treat a **full-service** rent as if triple-nets were separate, then decline over a rent cap they misunderstood. The more you translate the deal for them, the fewer bad declines. (Same principle as spelling a use out for a listing agent instead of trusting them to relay it.)
- **The "rent cap" is a green-light line, not a ceiling.** It means "we'll approve up to this *without question*" — you can exceed it with justification. Example: a space offering **$80/SF TI instead of $50** means the bank lends less, so you can run rent a bit over the cap. Understand this so a client isn't scared off by a number that's actually negotiable.
- **Give the underwriter a concrete number, not an open question.** They won't negotiate against themselves ("what's the most rent you'd allow?"). Bring *"the landlord will agree to $9,500/mo — thumbs up or down?"* — something they can approve.
- **Bounce counters off underwriting before you send them** (Brad): "can you check with underwriting — what if we did this?" so you don't send an LOI and have to retrade it.

## Creative lease structuring to pass underwriting
When the bank's rent cap is below what the landlord wants:
- **Front-load low, catch up later.** Bank caps years 1–2 at $10k but landlord wants $13k → write the lease at $10k for years 1–2, then either the client **writes a catch-up check at the start of year 3** (~$70k) or you **amortize the gap with interest** over the remaining term. Bank sees compliant early rent; landlord may *prefer* it (more total money, with interest); client gets the space; and the agent earns more (higher rents). 
- **Show the landlord the lender stipulation.** Frame the low early rent as *"the lender requires this — it's not us being greedy,"* and send the **pre-approval document** (Brad had a landlord ask to see it, then agree). His deal: got retail rent to $18/SF year 1 (asking was $38), $32 year 2, normal escalation after, plus an **extra year on the back end** (an 11-year deal) so the numbers penciled for everyone.
- **Brad's startup spreadsheet.** Plug in suite size, TI contribution/SF, estimated build-out cost, and total loan amount → it shows whether the loan's **buckets** (build-out / equipment / working capital) actually cover the space (a $950k loan can't build 3,000 SF at $230/SF). Output a PDF, send it to underwriting ("here's what I'm estimating — does this work?"), and it gives you **ammo back to the landlord** ("the numbers don't work — we need more TI"). Also stops you touring spaces that are too big.

## Get the lender involved EARLY ("real-estate 101" — Evan)
- **No tours without proof of funds or a pre-approval letter.** Like a residential broker won't show $3M homes without pre-approval — *"I'm too busy to show property until you show me the pre-approval; and if you can't get approved, you can't start your business."* Touring oversized/out-of-parameter space makes you look like you don't control the deal.
- **On the phone, qualify with nuance:** "Do you have family money? Do you know what it costs to open an office like this? Do you know what rents are here?" — educate them, then require the approval.
- **When a lender *brings* you the deal**, the client is pre-vetted and serious (they filled out bank forms). When a lead comes from elsewhere, pull a lender in fast.

## The wrong bank can kill a deal (Mike)
- **Overselling:** a banker says "of course we can do that," then can't at the finish (classic with SBA mis-structured as a 504). 
- **Scaring the client off:** ask "can you do startups?" and hear *"yeah, we've got a great SBA product"* → that banker doesn't understand healthcare and can **terrify a doctor out of a good startup.** War story: the wrong bank had a client spend $60k on architect/engineering, then said "cut $400k," killed the deal, and the partnership **broke up nasty.** Getting the right bank in front of the client is itself part of the job.

## Communication through the deal (Evan / Brad)
- Not much is needed after approval, but **hold the lease until you get written confirmation the bank is funding** (don't let a lease get signed before the bank has followed through). Get them engaged ~**1 month before closing**.
- **Defer to whoever controls the deal:** if the banker referred it, they may want to make the contractor/vendor intros — ask before bringing your own people in.

## Purchase & ground-up — the due-diligence question is #1 (Brad)
Before making an offer, ask the lender: **"How long do you need for due diligence, and what do you need in hand to close?"** It varies enormously by bank:
- **Live Oak (SBA 7A)** — the flexible one: will close a build-out purchase **without permits or a full set of CDs** — just a sketched plan + bids to reach the **as-complete appraisal**. (Also did an SBA *lease* loan and an SBA build-out that no conventional bank would touch — see the vet story below.) *Note: Live Oak is already in `vendors.xlsx` as `V-BNK-045`; this session strongly validates it as the go-to creative SBA lender.*
- **Wells Fargo** — historically required **final permits in hand** (7–8 months); now relaxed to requiring **full construction drawings submitted + an initial county response**. Either way the **client spends money up front** (pays the architect's full fee before closing).
- **Two appraisals on SBA build-out loans:** an **as-is** (worth today) and an **as-complete** (worth built out, with bids in hand). Every SBA lender wants both.
- **Match the DD period to the lender and market.** Competitive market: 60 days is too much (offer 45); ground-up needs 90+. Structure it as *"initial 60 days, plus a 30-day extension if required in writing by the lender"* — reads better than 90 up front. **Buy more DD time by making earnest money go hard** (Brad released $25k of $50k to get 45 extra days; sellers accept it because they keep something if the buyer walks).
- **Ground-up is a different animal:** subdividing a parcel, entitlement, a PD zoning change + special-use permit (e.g. no veterinary use-by-right in the town of Parker), town halls — can take **a year**. Investigate the local process *before* signing a 90-day purchase agreement, or the lender won't fund and the client loses earnest money.
- **When SBA makes sense:** on expensive deals, yes. Buying an **existing built-out building** → the **504** can be competitive with conventional, but 504 regs are thick; the **SBA 7A (Live Oak)** is more workable.

## Seller financing (Evan — the fast, bank-free path)
When you want to move fast, the client has cash, and you want to skip the bank timeline:
- **Structure:** the client puts up a chunk (10–50%; Evan's current deal is 50%), you agree on an interest rate, and it's a **promissory note attached to the purchase & sale contract** (both attorneys involved). No bank means **no zoning approval, no appraisal, no lender diligence period** — it's a contract between two people. **Close in ~2 weeks**, basically a cash deal.
- **Why a seller says yes** (the pitch to the listing side): **spreads their capital gains** (Evan's example — a seller who bought a $3M building "in the '60s for blueberries" faces a $2.5M gain; if the client puts up half, only ~$1M is taxable now), the seller **earns an interest rate** on the financed half, and there's usually a **balloon** (3–5 yr term on a 20–25 yr amortization) so the client refis to a traditional lender later. If the client defaults, the seller owns the property — **the seller is the bank** (foreclosure).
- **Term vs. amortization:** a 20-yr amortization with a 5-yr *term* means the loan balloons at year 5 with 15 years of payments still owed. Get the longest term and amortization you can.
- **⚠️ The diligence caveat (Scott/Mike):** you may skip the *appraisal*, but you must still **advise in writing** on zoning, survey, and title — because when the client later refis with a bank, a hidden issue means no bank will lend (and it's hard to resell). On a bank deal the lender does all this diligence *for* you and knows where the pitfalls are; on a cash/seller-financed deal that liability shifts, so **put the recommended checks in writing** (Agent Central's "cash deal quick" checklist covers what's needed). Don't downplay it.

## Bank referral fees & timing (panel Q&A)
Referral fees on lender introductions are **big and under-collected** — track when you refer a bank and follow up. And get the bank in **early** so they tell you what they'll need in 2–3 years: don't set a **2-year** seller-financing balloon if the bank needs **3 years** to refi the client out (or the client's stuck). The bank can also hand you a target **rate** — 15% to a seller isn't good if an 8% SBA is achievable even when conventional was declined.

## Recourse reality by deal size (merged 2026-07-31 from a $60MM-AUM acquirer's scaling thread — investor-side source, mined for the owner-occupier education our clients actually need)

The landscape, plainly: **in the sub-$5MM range where practice real estate lives, lenders want personal guarantees** — banks, SBA, credit unions all require recourse at this size; non-recourse debt (agency, CMBS) starts far above a practice acquisition. What this means for a doctor buying their building, translated for the client conversation: (1) a personal guarantee is NORMAL at this size, not a red flag or a negotiation failure — walking in knowing that prevents the deal-killing surprise at term sheet; (2) the honest risk math travels: at typical leverage the building value must fall past the equity cushion before the guarantee means anything personally — real risk, bounded risk, worth understanding rather than fearing; (3) guarantee TERMS still negotiate even when the guarantee itself doesn't — burn-off provisions (the PG releases at stabilization or a DSCR threshold), carve-outs, spousal-signature scope are all asks a represented buyer makes and an unrepresented one never hears about. Route the specifics through the lender (Jon Shepley lane) and the client's attorney — our seat frames the landscape, never papers it.
