# Part A — Valuing property
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from a CARR agent-training FAQ call (`training.carr.us/sessions/agent-training-march-2021`, titled "Valuing Property"; audio, ~24 min; presenter a senior CARR agent). Answers the deal-staller: "how do I know what a building/space is worth and what to offer?" The valuation companion to `negotiation.md`, and it leans on the same tools — the comparison spreadsheets (`../deal-analysis-toolkit.md` + `purchase-vs-lease-fill-guide.md`) and CoStar comps (`DNA/Research/costar-how-to.md`). Paraphrase-only per the Jul 8 policy: no transcript stored, timestamps not retained; the source session is identified in the capture ledger.*

> **Two principles under everything:**
> 1. **True value = what the seller will sell for AND what the buyer will pay** — there's no other "real" number.
> 2. **A reasonable offer gets a reasonable response; an unreasonable offer gets an unreasonable response — or none at all**.

---

## Purchases

- **An appraisal is NOT "the value" — its job is to justify the loan to the bank**. Don't let a low appraisal spook the client into blowing a good deal — Kevin saw a deal die over a **$30–40k appraisal shortfall** when walking away cost the client **~$500k over 10 years**. **Prep the client in advance** that the appraisal may land below the purchase price, and that it doesn't mean it's a bad deal.
- **The three appraisal methods** (so you can explain them):
  1. **Comparables** — similar sales in ~1–2 years; the **most-weighted**. Hard in healthcare: real estate is only **~50–60% of the project cost** (TI/build-out/equipment are the rest), and comparing a core-and-shell to a fully-built-out space is apples-to-oranges; supply/demand distorts it. Use **CoStar's Sales Comps** tab.
  2. **Income approach** — value if leased/as an investment; little weight for our deals.
  3. **Cost / replacement** — ground + building + finishes + construction time.
- **The #1 way CARR actually values a purchase = the Purchase-vs-Lease comparison spreadsheet** — it shows value **to THIS client vs. their real alternatives**, not an abstract market number. It's for you as much as the client. Key drivers: upfront/monthly cost (if it's similar-or-slightly-higher than leasing, buying often wins), tax benefits, and **principal pay-down (huge)**.
- **The assessor's website is one of the WORST ways to value** — every county values differently and many cap annual increases; it doesn't reflect real value.
- **Summary:** don't let a client blow up a $500k decision over the last $10–50k. Frame the long-term ownership-vs-lease picture.

### Two war-stories (why "the best price" ≠ "the best decision")
- **The $1.5M only-option building**: client insisted on low-balling ~$1M → **no response**; bumped to $1.15M → still nothing; ~30% under ask **burned the goodwill** (the seller had done their diligence and knew the value). The spreadsheet showed the purchase payment ≈ their current $15k/mo lease, with a **~$600–700k 10-year equity swing** in favor of buying. The client's fixation on "a deal" nearly cost the big picture.
- **The $270 vs. $190/SF condo**: client had 4 yrs left on a lease and an option to buy their built-out condo at $270/SF; balked because a condo down the street was $190/SF. But that $190 is a warm-vanilla shell needing ~$125/SF build-out = **$315/SF** — *plus* he'd still owe ~$250k in rent over his remaining 4 years. So the "$80/SF overcharge" was actually the cheaper, better deal. **"What's it worth to OUR client?" — the spreadsheet answers it.**

## Leases

- **Tell clients to ignore advertised lease rates** — they may be teaser rates; term, TI, and free rent are all unknown until you negotiate. **Pre-qualify the property** to learn whether the landlord can help a good long-term tenant with TI. On tour, judge properties on what actually matters: visibility, location, access, signage, aesthetics, parking, co-tenants.
- **Determining a reasonable lease value:** for a startup, **year-one rent must fit the loan parameters** (find the lender's cap). Get proposals on 2–4 properties and compare them in the spreadsheets. Comps are one input; also the Agent-Central video "Understanding the Landlord's Side of Negotiating."
- **The reasonable-offer rule applies to leases too**: a counter that guts the total cost of the deal (e.g. $1M → $600k over 10 yrs by piling on TI + cutting escalations + cutting the rate) isn't reasonable — understand the landlord's return.
- **Renewals:** rates are climbing (leases signed ~10 yrs ago in the recession). Even if the rate goes $30 → $33/SF, CARR is still getting the best terms possible — being involved isn't "costing" the client, and **the best outcome sometimes still means the rate goes up.**

## The one-line summary
Don't get hung up on the comp, the appraisal, or the assessor. **Find what the property is worth to YOUR client — the 10-year ownership-vs-lease picture — and that's the true value**. Your job is the best terms possible, even if the rate rises.

# Part B — Due diligence (buildings & land)
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from a CARR agent-training FAQ call (`training.carr.us/sessions/agent-training-faq-due-dilligence`, audio, ~55 min; presenter a senior CARR agent). The most-asked FAQ — the full DD process for purchasing a building or land, why each step exists, and how to size the timeline. Deal-execution reference; the negotiation companion is `negotiation.md`, and comps/zoning research runs through `DNA/Research/costar-how-to.md`. Paraphrase-only per the Jul 8 policy: no transcript stored, timestamps not retained; the source session is identified in the capture ledger. (Also on CARR Agent Central as the "purchase contracts checklist" + quick-part emails — the durable checklist source; this digest is the reasoning behind it.)*

> **The point of DD is to protect the CLIENT** — not just satisfy a lender. **Even cash buyers with no lender requiring anything should still do it** (put the recommendation in writing — see the bottom). The war-stories below are all clients who skipped it.

## The master method: start with the end, work backwards from the lender

1. **Ask the LENDER first**: **conventional vs. SBA?** — dramatically different timelines. SBA has many requirements (wants everything done so you can "dig the next day"); conventional is more flexible (he's seen land deals need only an *opinion of value*, no appraisal).
2. Ask exactly what's required: **survey vs. ILC** (Improvement Location Certificate — less in-depth); survey type (topography?); appraisal inputs; and **how long after the appraisal to close** (the LOI assumes 14 days).
3. **Work backwards to size the due-diligence period.** Ground-up SBA example: appraisal is ordered LAST → it needs a line-item contractor bid → which needs a full plan set (**4–6 wks**) → which needs a floor plan from the architect/Patterson (**2–3 wks**) → plus the appraisal itself (**2–3 wks**) ≈ **11 weeks / ~90 days**, before adding a survey. **Never fire off the purchase LOI with a price and a default DD window** — you'll short-side the client (rezoning 1 of 7 acres won't fit in 60 days, and "what were you thinking?" follows).
4. **You are the QUARTERBACK**: constantly ask "whose court is the ball in?" and push every party — if you sit back, it falls on the client.

## LOI-stage items
- **Owner's policy — ask the SELLER to pay**. Two title policies exist: **owner's** (buyer or seller pays) and **lender's**. If the same title company does both, the second is cheap (~$1,500 first / ~$150 second); a different company = paying ~$1,500 twice. (His LOI wording: "seller to provide most recent Phase One and survey if they have it, and clean title backed by owner's policy.")
- **Flag oddities now**: incorrect zoning / rezoning (esp. vet), cutting up a parcel (in Alabama: certified mail to neighbors within ~500 ft + a public meeting), parking. Find these at the LOI stage, not late.
- **DD length is a two-way lever**: don't ask for 150 days you won't use — it *weakens* your offer (deadly against multiple offers or on a hot condo). A **shorter DD can win a competitive deal**. If you need longer, give the seller *color* (going SBA, here's why) so they don't think you're holding it off-market; or use a **30-day extension option**.

## Know every party (so you know whose court the ball is in)
- **Title company** — whoever pays picks it; if the seller picks, confirm the **lender approves** that title company. **Escrow agent** (who holds funds — ideally under the title company). **Closing attorney** — usually neutral. Plus **buyer & seller counsel**, and **architect / contractor / space planner / engineer / inspector**.

## Process — buying a BUILDING
1. **Earnest/escrow money** to the escrow agent.
2. **Order the title commitment** (get the purchase contract to title/escrow/closing attorney) — ~1–2 wks; surfaces easements/title issues.
3. **Inspections — the AGENT does NOT order them**. Give the client **2–3 inspectors** (liability: never just one — if your single pick misses something, the client can come at you). Report → pull the contractor to price the hot-button items → **"inspection objection"** reopens the negotiation (e.g. $50k roof → ask $30k off).
4. **Title work review**: **endorsements** (what they'll insure) vs. **exceptions** (what they won't — access, parking, easements). Send to the lender.
5. **ALTA survey exception**: title only covers *public records*, not what's physically on the land — no ALTA survey = an ALTA exception the bank usually won't accept → **get a survey.** A survey is a pictorial depiction of the title work *and* shows what's actually there — e.g. a neighbor's parking lot encroaching, requiring a **quiet title** to prevent an adverse-possession claim. Survey: ~2–4 wks.
6. **Phase One** (environmental) — lender may require, esp. on land / main thoroughfares. **AL & FL sellers don't have to disclose**, so more Phase Ones there. Checks historical records for a former gas station/laundromat; evidence → **Phase Two** (soil samples); contamination → expensive mitigation (dig out & replace dirt).
7. **Feasibility study — can the soil hold a building?** Banks usually DON'T require it, but the GC will — **do it on every ground deal.** War-stories: a filled-in former lake needed 6 ft dug out + steel mesh; another site was rock → **$1.4M just to get pad-ready.**
8. **Property insurance** (near the end); **condo** → get the **CC&Rs** (Codes, Covenants & Restrictions) reviewed by the attorney. **Off-record matters** — ask the seller for all docs, leases, estoppels (any tenant with an out on sale?).
9. **Appraisal — usually LAST**. Two kinds: **as-is** vs. **as-built** (will it appraise for the price + build-out, e.g. +$150/SF for a vet office?). **The as-built appraisal is CARR's recurring problem** — appraisers underestimate medical build-out cost. Head it off: ask who's doing it and offer build-cost comps (banks may bar contact with the appraiser). If it won't appraise and there's no room in the loan → client pays out of pocket, renegotiate, or walk.
10. **Loan approval / financing** (last). **Track critical dates & deadlines**: get a critical-dates document from the attorney, calendar the DD expiration **a week+ out**, and have any extension conversation **early** (the seller may demand more money down or money going hard). Never at 90 minutes to deadline.

## Buying LAND — same process, more of it
Survey; engineer plans; city approval (ingress/egress, turn lanes); rezoning; Phase One more likely; **feasibility study imperative.** Architect/engineer/contractor drive the front end — check in **weekly**. Watch for **extra approval boards** (historic districts, development ARBs) beyond the city.

## Zoning — ask the RIGHT question
Not "does this **zoning** allow dental?" but **"is [use] an allowable use on THIS PARCEL?"** — and get it in **writing** (a ~$12 zoning letter). Don't rely on CoStar; call the city. War-stories: healthcare was zoned but the specific use needed a variance; a vet couldn't be within 200 ft (parcel-border to parcel-border) of a restaurant — 6 ft short → variance.

## Cash buyers & the "cover yourself" rule
When there's no lender requiring DD, **still send the same recommendation email and put everything in writing** — the lender normally protects the client (by protecting itself), so without one the client is exposed and it can **fall back on you**. Get the "are we good on the soil?"-type questions answered **in writing**, not by phone.

## Commission protection (INTERNAL — firewall)
Even where the commission is built into the purchase contract (e.g. FL), still get a **commission agreement (CA) from the seller** — CARR isn't a party to the purchase contract, so without it you're exposed to being cut out ("buyer and seller cut Joe out for 3% off"). *This is internal/commission-firewall material — never client-facing, never content.*

# Part C — Space-type advisory: office vs retail
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from the CARR 2026 National Meeting producer panel (a producer panelist's argument, echoed by the founder). A client-advisory reference for the "what KIND of space?" conversation — distinct from own-vs-lease (`purchase-vs-lease-fill-guide.md`) and from valuation (`diligence-and-valuation.md`). Use it when a client is defaulting to prime retail and an office suite may serve them better. **This is genuinely client-facing advice (the client-benefit case comes first); the commission upside is real but secondary and never the pitch.***

## The core advice
For **~9 of 10 healthcare practices**, a professional **office suite beats prime retail** on both cost and quality of space. Many doctors default to retail for visibility and end up **overpaying to compete for the same corners as national brands** (Starbucks, Chipotle, banks) — a fight a single practice usually loses on price.

## The client-benefit case (lead with this)
- **Roughly half the rent.** Comparable office space frequently runs about half the base rate of prime retail in the same submarket.
- **The all-in gap is wider than the base rent shows.** On a true-triple-net **retail** lease, add ~**$2/SF utilities** + ~**$1.50/SF janitorial** (trash-out, wipe-down) on top of base — call it **$3.50–4/SF** that's often bundled in an office deal. Factor that in when comparing.
- **Better space quality.** Better window lines and natural light; retail boxes are often deep, narrow "bowling alleys." (Exception noted below: optometrists may *not* want windows in exam lanes.)
- **Marketing beats signage.** Take the rent savings and put them into **targeted marketing you can turn on and off** — it drives more new patients than a sign on a busy road, and you're not paying the visibility premium for 15–30 years. (CARR marketing partners referenced on the call: Wondrous, et al.)
- **You're competing against weaker bidders.** For an office suite you're usually up against a random tech or CPA firm — not a national retailer with deeper pockets and a must-have-this-corner mandate. Pair that with the **blue-chip-healthcare-tenant story** (10–15 yr term, <1% default rate) and the practice is the landlord's preferred bet.

## The caveat — it's 9 of 10, not all
Retail genuinely wins for:
- **Veterinary** (not going into an office tower).
- **Retail-brand / walk-in models** — e.g. Aspen-Dental-type brands built around street visibility.
- Uses that live or die on **impulse walk-in traffic**.
Don't force the office argument on these. The point is "don't *assume* prime retail is the right or affordable answer," not "never retail."

## The commission reality (INTERNAL — never part of the client pitch)
It also happens to be a better deal for the agent, which is why it's an easy alignment: office landlords typically pay ~**5%** (and will do **15-yr terms** with modest ~2% annual bumps and more TI), while **retail pays 2.5–3%** and pays it **slowly, in thirds** (first third often at rent commencement ~8 months out). Steering a client to office where it genuinely serves them can turn a ~$30–35k retail commission into ~$90–100k — but the deciding factor presented to the client is always *their* economics and space quality. This paragraph stays internal.

## How to use it in a deal
1. When a client opens with "I want a retail spot on [busy road]," don't argue — **run both**. Model the retail option and a comparable office option side by side (the Lease Comparison template handles up to 4 properties), all-in including the retail NNN utility/janitorial load.
2. Show the rent delta, then reframe the delta as a **marketing budget** ("that $5–8k/month difference, turned into targeted patient acquisition you control").
3. Sanity-check the use against the 9-of-10 caveat before pushing office.
4. For a longer office term, make sure the TI and escalations are right (see `negotiation.md` and `financing.md` for the TI/rent-cap mechanics).

# Part D — Complex-deal execution judgment
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from CARR's "Complex Deals" 2026 National Meeting panel — a producer panelist (Columbus OH; land & ground-up), a producer panelist (Philadelphia; investment purchases + vet zoning), a producer panelist (Philadelphia; practice acquisitions). The deals "outside the meat and potatoes" — more moving parts, more parties, bigger dollars ($1.8–2.5M+). Complements `diligence-and-valuation.md` (the DD *process* for buying), `financing.md` (lender mechanics + seller financing), and the renewal/landlord playbooks. **Amounts are illustrative Midwest/Northeast examples — directional.** Mostly INTERNAL agent craft; two doctor-facing content angles broken out at the end.*

> **What makes a deal "complex":** it's not a standard lease or a board-of-realtors purchase. It's the number of parties, their egos, and their differing knowledge levels — and your job is to know where everyone is and keep it all moving forward. Most of what the client never sees you do is exactly this.

---

## Part 1 — Land purchase & ground-up (a producer panelist)

### Timeline & the lender's checklist
- **Budget 90 / 120 / 150 days and assume you'll need an extension.** The lender drives it: **as-complete appraisal, zoning, environmental, survey, title** all have to clear before they'll fund. SBA is slower. Sellers want 30 days — flat-out impossible on a ground-up.
- **Blame the lender to the seller (usefully):** "This isn't me slow-playing it — I'd close tomorrow too; my hands are tied by the lender's process, and so are the buyer's." Knowing the checklist lets you deflect the pressure honestly.
- **Extension leverage (the renewal-specialist producer's signature move, from his banking days): always show progress.** At day 89, go back to the seller for a 30-day extension **with the bank's completed checklist in hand** — proves best foot forward, the delays were the survey company / the builder's as-built drawings, not you. Sellers grant extensions when they see the effort.

### Utilities — the negotiable Kyle learned the hard way
**Who brings utilities (electric, water, sewage) to the pad site is negotiable — find out EARLY.** If they're not already on the site: push it as the **seller's cost**, or get a **price break in lieu** ($20–50k typical). One deal required boring under a street to reach the site — **$80k, and it nearly killed the deal.** A grass lot that "looks great" from the sidewalk can hide this. Check first.

### The as-complete appraisal — and get involved with the appraiser
- A ground-up loan needs an **as-complete** appraisal (value once the building + equipment are in), not just as-is — and that takes time; builders sometimes resist producing what's needed, but it's required.
- **Appraisers often don't understand the value of a dental/medical build-out** → the appraisal can come in low. As the vertical expert, **pull comps yourself** ("here are the last five ground-up dental projects in central Ohio, here's how they finished vs. the market") and **give them to the appraiser** to hold the value up. The client never sees this; it keeps the deal alive. *(Extends the as-is/as-built appraisal material in `diligence-and-valuation.md`.)*

### Financing structures for holding land now, building later
- **Bridge loan:** the bank bridges the **land purchase** (conventional, not SBA), giving 6–12 months before breaking ground.
- **Pull equity from the existing practice** to collateralize, buy the land, then slow-play the build-out over 12–18 months. *(See the practice-equity mechanic in `financing.md`.)*
- **Cash land purchase (client has 4–5 yrs left on a lease, won't let the parcel slip):** a 30–60 day close, no lender checklist — **BUT still do zoning, soil boring, environmental, and survey during due diligence.** Don't let a client sink $250–300k of cash into land only to discover an environmental issue that blocks the future refi/build loan → unbuildable, unfinanceable land. These four checks are the non-negotiable protection on a cash land buy.

### Built-to-suit (contractor-run ground-up)
- The **contractor** buys the land, gets bids (core/shell → interior build-out → opening day), then **backs out the lease rate** they need (often high — "not for the faint of heart"). Don't lead with "let's do a built-to-suit" — it discredits you; it's not right for most clients.
- **Right client:** one who explicitly asks for it (rare), or a market with **great demographics but no real estate to lease or buy.** The contractor builds it their way on their dime, then **sells it back to the doctor in ~36–48 months** (baked into the lease). A means to an end.
- **Getting paid (Columbus example):** you're paid **on the land** (the contractor/developer is your client there) **and again on the lease.**
- **⚠️ Compliance — the "developer end-up" disclosure:** when you rep the developer on the land purchase but plan to rep a *different* client on the lease/purchase, there's a required disclosure creating that distinction ("we represent you the developer on the land; we plan to represent a different client on the lease-with-purchase"). **Nationwide best practice; NOT in Salesforce — get it from Scott.**

### Seller financing
Reinforced here, but fully captured in `financing.md` — the seller carries 20–25%, ~20-yr amortization, 5-yr balloon, negotiable interest, done as a promissory note with an attorney attached to closing. **You still get paid on the total purchase price, like any deal.** See that playbook for the full mechanics.

## Part 2 — Investment purchases & the doctor-as-investor play (a producer panelist)

### The investor mindset
Any investment = **rate of return × risk × time.** The variables (NOI, cap rate) are easy; **the skill is identifying and reducing risk.** True outside investors are hard to transact with (financing is the wall — Pete went "along for the ride" with a young MBA investor and it went nowhere).

### The value-add play doctors can run (the useful part)
A doctor can borrow the investor mindset to turn a practice purchase into a near-term investment:
- **Buy a building bigger than the practice needs.** Example: a 10,000 SF building, 5,000 SF already tenanted, 5,000 vacant; the practice takes ~3,000 SF. Sitting half-vacant, an outside investor prices **high risk** on it.
- **The doctor tenants it himself** (signs his practice to a **long-term lease with good guarantees**) → **NOI jumps** → the building becomes far more appealing to investors. Fill the remaining space with another (ideally healthcare) tenant and NOI maximizes.
- **Exit early:** after just **3–4 years** of establishing himself as a solid long-term tenant, the doctor can **cash out on the real estate** — instead of the traditional "run the practice 15–20 years, then sell the building." The lease-up is what shortens the timeline.
- **Financing reality:** SBA needs **51% owner-occupancy** (agents get creative counting storage/break rooms toward the 51%); under ~50% it's conventional with more down, or non-traditional lenders on strong personal financials. Not easy — which is *why* the opportunity exists.
- **The doctor's edge:** knowing what makes a location good for *healthcare* tenants (highway access, parking, proximity to hospital systems) lets them **preemptively reduce the risk** a future investor-buyer would price in — both by tenanting the vacancy and by choosing the right location/condition up front.

### Zoning & entitlement — the political navigation (esp. veterinary)
- **The caveat first (CARR rule, per Mike's email):** we are **NOT zoning attorneys.** We gather information, cannot guarantee a use is approved, and must tell clients to consult an attorney. *(Reinforces the zoning caveat in `diligence-and-valuation.md`.)*
- **Vet is often mis-coded or absent** in old (1960s–70s) zoning written when vets meant farms/large-animal — so a small-animal hospital may not be classed as medical/professional office, or isn't listed at all.
- **Take the township's temperature BEFORE hiring a land-use attorney.** Call the township — even the lowest-level person who picks up — to gauge: is this **NIMBY-galore where everything's a fight**, or a **business-friendly township** that wants to make it work? You want accuracy, but mostly you want the *read*.
- **Local land-use attorneys are political** (they're in front of the same boards repeatedly; they win some, lose some, trade, and protect their own credibility). One killed a deal ("you'll never get the use here") when a **more nimble, surface-level investigation** — talking to the mayor/council directly — might have gone differently. It's not a courtroom, so **direct contact with elected officials isn't ex parte.**
- **Go over a wrong rejection to the right person.** War story: a **clerk wrongly rejected** a use; going to a newly-elected, business-friendly **mayor** ("I think they misinterpreted this") produced an email the **next day** — "clerk was wrong, use approved." A 3–6 month process cleared in a day.
- **Avoid the zoning hearing at all costs.** Zoning *officers* have little discretion and tend to be conservative to be safe; a formal hearing (esp. New Jersey / brutal townships) is where deals die. When the initial rejection or ambiguity comes, find the local politicians, learn the sensitivities, and work people of influence to move the ball.

## Part 3 — Practice-acquisition lease negotiation (a producer panelist)

When a doctor **buys a practice** that has a lease in place, the real-estate negotiation is a distinct, often-quick, high-ROI deal.

### The landscape
- **Practice brokers** (DDS Match is strongest in many markets; BofA has a separate acquisition group) mostly **don't want us involved** — they want to sell the practice, take their ~10%, and get out; they don't care about the rent. Hard to break in — show your worth. (one producer does 3–5 *paid* acquisitions/year plus many **pro bono** — see below.) They mostly **rep listings only**, not buyers ("a spider web waiting for flies"). Trade referrals with them over time (startups, "glass-slipper" matches).

### The lease play (it's a renewal in disguise)
- **The buyer needs enough lease term to match the loan length**, so extending term is the lever. **Less existing term = more leverage** (posture like a renewal / chart sale). With **1–3 years left** you can push; with **7–8 years left** the landlord says "talk to me in eight years."
- **The posture:** when the landlord dares you to move ("they'll buy this practice no matter what, it'll cost you $500k to relocate"), counter with **"there are four other built-out dental spaces I can slide them into"** — being the known local dental agent, they can't prove otherwise.
- **Case study (Sally Gupton, Fort Washington PA):** 30-year tenant, wildly over-market rent, nets ~$20/SF; **four acquisition deals had already died on the rent.** With ~12 months left on the lease, the landlord knew a vacancy meant a brokerage commission + new TI + 12–18 months of rental interruption + *lower* market rent than the buyer would pay → deal got done. **Saved the buyer ~$550k in rent; CARR paid ~$30k.** ~10 hours of work — "a couple of emails and knowing the market."

### Two high-value insights from acquisitions
- **Cutting the rent makes the practice worth MORE.** Buyers pay on **existing overhead** (an EBITDA multiple), so artificially inflated rent is baked into the price. Reduce rent ~30% and **the practice is worth more overnight** because fixed overhead drops. This is a purchase-and-sale value lever, not just a savings.
- **Seller carry-back on the building** (audience add): if the *selling* doctor holds the real estate and **carries back the loan** instead of selling the building outright, they **defer their capital gains** significantly while the buyer's practice bank lends, and the buyer buys the building out in ~12–18 months. A win for the seller's tax bill. *(Related to seller financing in `financing.md` Part B.)*

### INTERNAL — commission posture on short-term acquisition leases
**⚠️ INTERNAL-ONLY (commission handling — never client-facing, never content).**
- **Don't put a 10-year commission on a deal that only had 2 years of term.** Tell the landlord you're **only asking commission on the NEW rent/term you're adding**, not the rent already committed to — "so you don't look like a pig going in," and the landlord finds you reasonable and pays more readily.
- **The pro-bono comps/BOV play:** CPAs/lawyers call for a quick "what should the rent be here?" → run a fast CoStar range for free. It generates goodwill that turns into startup referrals later. (Same tactic as the "free comps way into practice brokers" in `DNA/Leads/pipeline-craft.md` and `DNA/Network/vendor-relationship-craft.md`.)
- **Philosophy split (noted, not prescriptive):** one producer *won't* insert himself for a commission when the selling doc holds/sells the building (the commission just gets passed to the buyer-doc and annoys the referrer) — he orchestrates from behind the scenes, feeds his title/attorney vendors, and collects via later referrals. Kyle "bakes it in and charges." Two valid approaches.

# Part E — The per-deal workup (the orchestrator step, added 2026-07-20)

Parts B and D are the FULL menu for every deal type. But which items matter is deal-specific and unknowable until you see the deal — a ground-up land build and an existing-building purchase need different work. So don't run the whole menu every time. Run this front step first: classify the deal, then pull ONLY the relevant workers into a tailored checklist for THIS deal.

Classify the deal type, then pull:
- **Existing building purchase** → Part B "Process — buying a BUILDING" + valuation (Part A / the PvL spreadsheet) + the zoning question (Part B) + the read-only dossier if ownership/title is unclear.
- **Land + ground-up** → Part B "Buying LAND" + Part D Part 1 (lender timeline, utilities, as-complete appraisal, financing structures) + entitlement/zoning politics (Part D Part 2, esp. veterinary).
- **Investment purchase (doctor-as-landlord)** → Part A income approach + Part D Part 2 (value-add play, investor mindset) + the sub-tenant TI handling in the PvL fill guide.
- **Practice-acquisition lease** → Part D Part 3 (lease-as-renewal, the two acquisition insights) + negotiate-before-you-own leverage (templates hook #10).
- **Lease-only (new / relocating practice)** → Part A "Leases" (ignore teaser rates, pre-qualify for TI) + a 2-4 property PvL compare + the vertical size band.

Then emit the tailored workup: only the relevant items, in lender-backwards order (Part B's master method), with the vertical guide's build-out specs and the specific zoning question for THIS use. Optionally spawn the read-only single-target dossier (upstream-radar.md) to pre-fill ownership/title/zoning before the human works it.

This turns a long reference menu into a per-deal investigation the session actually runs, without re-reading the whole playbook each time. Orchestrator-workers: classify the deal, delegate only the relevant workers. Stamp: Joe's brain, 2026-07-20.
