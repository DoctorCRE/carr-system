# Part A — Bankers & lenders (the #1 referral category)
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), distilled from CARR's EDU call "Lenders & Bankers" (a 15-year banker turned CARR agent). **Bankers are CARR's #1 referral source (~70% of leads; 46 banker records in `vendors.xlsx`), so this is the highest-leverage vendor relationship there is.** Verbatim material and timestamps not retained (paraphrase-only policy, July 8, 2026); the source session is identified in the Source Material ingestion ledger. (The call referenced an emailed Excel debt-service sheet + bankrate amortization calc — not captured; recreate from the DSR math below.)*

> **Mindset:** every bank *employee* is a potential referral partner (a "COI" — center of influence). Unlike supply reps, you'll never run out of banks/bankers to meet. And it's not a transactional deal-swap — CARR raises the bank's **conversion rate**, which is worth far more to them than a reciprocal lead.

## First meeting with a banker — make it about THEM
- Open with **their** goals, not real estate: *"What are you trying to accomplish this year, and how are you getting there?"*
- Then learn the bank's **appetite / competitive advantage** — the questions that let you speak their language:
  - 100% financing? Practice loans (dental/medical/vet)? What client types do they love?
  - **Term length** — old-school banks cap at 7–10 yr; ~15 lenders now do 15-yr terms (or amortize 15 / fix 10); some do 20–25 yr; a few do **25-yr fixed under 5%**. (Illustration: $2.5M at 4.9%, 25-yr = ~$8,333/mo — can be *less than rent*. Rates dated — reset.)
  - Ground-up construction financing? Is construction **monitoring** required (adds client cost)?
  - **Lending limits** ($1.5M / $3M / $6M?).
  - Will they issue a **pre-approval letter**? (A FL municipality wanted a letter of credit before zoning.)
  - Do they require a **landlord waiver**? (one more lease-negotiation ask)
  - *"Can I broadcast your appetite to my clients and referral partners?"* — cuts through the BS and implies "we're going to do business."

## Deepening an existing banker relationship
- Early each year, ask their **goals for the year — with the implication you'll help hit them.** Example: a Tampa banker said *"lending isn't my goal — I crushed it. I need DEPOSITS. Connect me with attorneys who run escrow/IOLTA accounts."* Nothing to do with borrowers; you only learn it by asking — and it changes the whole dynamic.

## How CARR adds value to the BANK (this is the pitch)
- **We raise their conversion rate**: a bank can approve a doctor easily, but with no space there's no deal — and doctors going solo hit roadblocks (landlords won't call back, they get preyed on, they give up). Inserting CARR gets deals across the line.
- **"Approve-not-closed"**: banks *grade lenders* on approved deals that don't close (worse than a decline — the onus lands on the lender). So **keep the banker informed on deal progress, flag when competitors are involved, and tell them what they must do to win.**
- **Only show the client properties that fit the bank's approval appetite** (asterisk: still show the client what they want — but keep looping back to referrers, or the referrals dry up; you can't keep showing unapprovable space).
- **Get the client approved EARLY** — deals go fast; early approval boosts CARR's cachet, gives the landlord comfort (a bank letter), adds an ally, and builds reciprocity. Keep the doctor sending financials so re-approval is fast (approvals expire; a stale one costs a 14–21 day wait). *(BofA quirk: they want the landlord to fund the TI allowance BEFORE the bank funds construction — address it early.)*

## Meet UP and DOWN the bank's org chart
- **Meet the banker's boss ASAP** — find out if the supervisor/area/regional manager has **exception power** (for clients exceeding the normal lending limit), so they already know CARR when you need an exception. Ask their underwriting rules and where they flex (e.g., a limit rises if the borrower moves deposits over — $1M normally, more with $150k on deposit).
- **Meet the people BELOW your banker** — find the **"apex influencer"** (a branch manager, closer, or admin with 100 local relationships feeding your banker). They may trust CARR more than they trust their own banker — you only discover that by meeting them.

## The relationship-building sequence (mirrors CARR's official notes)
1. **Every banker has someone to refer; the bank itself can be a client** (a Sarasota bank needed land for a new branch — Derek worked to win it from the incumbent listing firm).
2. Understand the bank's **credit process, appetite, and cash-flow requirements.**
3. On a **success, meet the banker's boss within 2–3 weeks** and explain how you won the deal together — celebrate the banker so they refer everyone, and the boss introduces the whole team.
4. **Home run: speak at the bank's area meeting** (they meet ≥1×/yr).
5. **Monthly follow-up email** — a real-estate-market note or a lease-savings story keeps you front of mind.
6. **The retention pitch (killer angle):** branch managers / business bankers are incentivized to **RETAIN clients**, and their fees are scrutinized. Framing: *"we saved your client $1,500/yr — that pays 100% of the fees your bank charges them for the year."* Even $110/mo on a renewal can cover a client's annual bank fees. Position CARR's savings as helping the banker **keep** their client → high referral likelihood.

## The Debt-Service Ratio (DSR) — 5-minute bank math to speak their language & save declined deals
- Banks divide **practice revenue ÷ annual loan payments (all debt) = the Debt-Service Ratio** (revenue-to-annual-debt).
- Compute the loan's annual debt: total it (principal + all future interest) via a **bankrate.com amortization calculator**. Rule of thumb: **every $1M at ~4% ≈ $350k interest over 10–15 yr**.
- Worked example: $2M RE loan → ~$2.7M total payments → **~$183k/yr** over 15 yr. Vet's income $250k → **$250k ÷ $183k = DSR 1.3** (strong).
- **Most banks require ~1.25** (the doctor makes $1.25 per $1 of debt); some are tighter, some lenders go as low as 1:1 (esp. expansions with new revenue).
- **Use it to rescue a decline**: if a lender declines, run the DSR — something may be missing from their equation, OR **find another lender with a looser ratio to approve the client whose space you already found** (don't waste the work).

# Part B — Supply & equipment reps (the largest category)
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), distilled from CARR's EDU call "Tailoring the Message — Merchandise Reps" (the ex-supply-rep trainer, 15 yrs clinical then a Henry Schein / Benco / Burkhart rep). **Supply/Equipment Reps are the LARGEST vendor category in `vendors.xlsx` (49 records) and a core referral source.** Sibling to `vendor-relationship-craft.md`. Verbatim material and timestamps not retained (paraphrase-only policy, July 8, 2026); the source session is identified in the Source Material ingestion ledger.*

> **The whole game is empathy → trust.** Reps are bombarded by vendors pushing product daily; you win by understanding their brutal job and showing how *you* help *them*, not by talking about how great CARR is (the ex-supply-rep trainer's own opening mistake).

## Understand the rep's world (so your pitch lands)
- The job is high-stress, high-turnover. the ex-supply-rep trainer was handed **300 accounts** told to "narrow to your best 100–150," and cried in her car after a week of slammed doors. Low pay, no car/expense allowance early on.
- Reps are the **"ringleaders"** — hunt the business and are the doctor's main point of contact for supplies, equipment, tech, and business solutions. Companies want them *consultative*, but the market is brutal: Amazon/gray-market price-checking, low-cost distributors, flat earnings.
- Doctors are hyper-particular — **the wrong gauze (or burr grit) can lose an account**; 50,000 products, each with parameters. The rep is "an office manager on steroids."
- The economics are thin: the ex-supply-rep trainer **grew her book 10% and made $20k less**; average rep margin **≤25%**, with 100+ accounts to service. So a rep who can make **$2,000 on a CBCT/chair sale in a 5-minute window will let your lease conversation take a back seat** — which is exactly why you frame CARR as helping them sell *more*.

## The CARR pitch to a rep (why working with you helps THEM)
CARR saves their client money on the lease (or relocates them → a chance to sell **more equipment/tech/supplies**), **points the savings back to the rep**, lets the rep **call the shots on which other vendors to bring in**, and provides **savings-summary letters**. Frame it as: *"I help your client free up cash and grow — which means more for you to sell."*

## Know the rep archetypes
- **Old-timers / "the legends"** — the pre-internet "milk run"; old, loyal accounts; won't change ("dog on the nail — it doesn't hurt bad enough"). Won't stir the pot.
- **Top-5 reps** — really get it, sell business solutions; **hardest to catch and to earn trust** (seen vendors come and go, been burned). They see your value but need time.
- **The average** — bare minimum ("10-to-2, Tue–Thu"), collecting a paycheck.
- **Rookies** — Schein/Patterson now hire young grads cheap; **~50% wash out in year one**, ~2–3 of 20 survive five years. They're fed the "bottom-feeder" open accounts, overwhelmed and undertrained. When they leave, those accounts feed the senior reps.

## How to work them
- **Small-group / 1:1 beats the branch meeting**. Throwing them a lead helps (but can stir branch politics).
- **When a rep says "give me ALL your leads"**: *"You already have 100 leads in your accounts — just ask them about their lease. I'll co-travel with you, or give me your top-5 list; ~20–30% will either move or I'll save them enough that they can buy from you."* (Same **"at-bat / shot at the deal"** ethic as the vendor-referral rules.)
- **Don't overlook the SERVICE team** — service techs literally have keys to the back doors of offices (deep trust); independent sterilizer-service reps have their own accounts. Find them; if you can't get into the sales-rep meeting, call the **operations manager**, get in with the service team, buy them lunch. They fed the ex-supply-rep trainer lots of new-office/expansion/equipment business — co-travel with them.
- **Earning trust took sacrifice**: meeting on Saturdays / on the rep's schedule. "When can you meet? I'll work around your schedule" — not "M–F, 8–5."

## Questions to ask a rep (first meeting)
- *"I know your schedule's tight seeing 10+ accounts — can I meet you at your first stop with coffee, 5–10 min, just to get to know each other?"* (their schedule, and **don't just talk about yourself** — learn their business/challenges).
- *"What's the best way you communicate new products to your doctors?"* (flyers/handouts → leave a CARR flyer; a newsletter → contribute an article/Q&A).
- *"Where are other vendors / direct companies taking deals away from you? Anyone I can connect you with to win it back?"*
- *"What part of your business do you want to grow this year?"* (if equipment/tech → find doctors needing expansions / an extra op).
- *"Want me to host a happy hour / dinner for your top offices as a customer-appreciation night?"* / *"Any doctors looking for study-club sponsorship?"*

## Q&A
- **Merch rep vs. equipment rep**: years ago equipment reps got the leads; now the doctor relies on the local **merch rep as the ringleader**, who brings the equipment person in. Bring the equipment rep in, but lead with the merchandise rep.
  - **⚠️ Qualified 2026-07-31 by a live case.** On the Brandvold C-153 deal a Patterson **Equipment & CAD/CAM Specialist** sourced the client, made the introduction to Dell, and is running the project himself. **Split the rule by deal type: lead with the merchandise rep on an ESTABLISHED account, lead with the equipment specialist on a PROJECT** (start-up, second office, relocation), because the equipment specialist owns the build and their commission is gated on the doctor securing space. Full channel breakdown, the per-operatory economics, and the Cirrus Consulting Group competitor finding: `vendor-by-industry-reference.md` §1.
- **Sponsoring the branch monthly meeting**: find the **branch admin** for a vendor slot — but it's hard (big suppliers like 3M book months out; small slots; the message is diluted by 11am after 15 vendors). Grab the early 8am slot if you can, but 1:1 tends to work better in busy markets.
- **Rep already loyal to another realtor**: sell the CARR-is-different story (lean on any healthcare background), then **ask for an at-bat** — *"I appreciate your loyalty; I'm just asking that you give both names and let the best person win for the client — it's an emotional process, your client deserves an option."*

# Part C — General vendor mastery
*Created 2026-07-07 by Claude Code (watch-video skill), from CARR's "Mastering Vendors 201" panel — Sam Goodrich (Utah, ex-dental-supply manager), an ex-Schein/Patterson equipment specialist in **North Alabama** (Joe's territory-adjacent — worked with a senior CARR agent), a West Michigan agent, and the mapping team Daniels (Raleigh NC, ~7 yrs). This is the **cross-cutting advanced layer** above the category playbooks (`vendor-relationship-craft.md`, `vendor-relationship-craft.md`): once you've met your ~200 vendors (the 101 move — see `DNA/Leads/pipeline-craft.md`), this is how you go from *asking for referrals* to *being the hub everyone routes through*. Internal agent-development craft — not client-facing, not content. Verbatim material and timestamps not retained (paraphrase-only policy, July 8, 2026); the source session is identified in the Source Material ingestion ledger.*

> **The through-line:** vendors take TIME. Sam's #1 referring partner wouldn't return his call for **two years**; now they talk 2–3×/week. His top partner ever (a practice consultant) literally **hung up on him** (she disliked CARR / his predecessor) — he did homework, left a voicemail distancing himself from the predecessor, and 3–4 months later she came around → **six startup deals that all closed** in his first two years. Hang in there; the payoff compounds.

## 1. Chase the vendors who DON'T call you back
Counterintuitive but core: **the vendors protective of their time are usually the ones whose time is most valuable — and who sit on the best deal information.** They're busy doing their job and networking, so they're hard to reach; it can take a year or two to get on their radar. Conversely, **be wary of the "chirping bird" vendor** who chases *you* — often they have no deals to give and just want your referrals. Be protective of your own time too; making yourself available to every vendor who wants a meeting isn't a recipe for success. You screen them as they screen you. (You must also *do* something before the good ones refer — chicken-and-egg; fight through it with "this is what CARR does," which isn't a lie.)

## 2. Stop asking for referrals — start CONNECTING vendors to each other (be the hub)
Sam's biggest level-up: he went from always asking vendors for referrals to **introducing his vendors to one another** — "do you know the Live Oak banker? do you know this contractor? let's all go to lunch." It became organic: the equipment specialist who ignored him for two years now calls *him* asking for contractor referrals. **"I like being viewed as the guy in the center of the team."** Introduce everybody to everybody — banker ↔ contractor ↔ CPA ↔ architect ↔ listing agent — and you become the connector nobody wants to be left out of.

## 3. Run vendor networking groups / facilitated lunches
Both Sam and the mapping team do this and rate it their most effective move.
- **Assemble a small standing group** — e.g. accountant + banker + contractor (Sam: 5–6 people); the mapping team's group is attorney + marketing + dental equipment rep + dental territory rep + lender + wealth management + IT. Meet **monthly (to quarterly by season)** at a nice spot (the mapping team uses Capital Grille — people will take a Friday off for it). Invite **association leaders** (NC Dental Society) and sometimes clients/doctors.
- **The ROI math is trivial:** Sam spent **$150 on pizza for 9 people** — one deal makes that a rounding error.
- **"Always invite the banker — the banker always pays."** If a bank rep is in the room, lunch is covered; otherwise be willing to foot the bill.
- **If you're not in a group, create one.** It solidifies you as the market's expert/hub, and people gravitate to you.

## 4. Bring your team into the deal EARLY (the team-lunch close)
When Sam gets a referral, he doesn't try to sign an ETL off a phone call — he takes the doctor to lunch **and brings his banker + contractor**. The pitch as a trio: *"You want a practice — we'll show you how to get it, how to pay for it, and how to build it."* It takes the weight off the doctor's shoulders, and **he's "never *not* had one sign an ETL"** done this way. Bonus effects: the banker hears the contractor present and vice-versa (they become a team around you), your referring-partner relationships solidify, and — the banker buys lunch. Introduce partners in early; it's a step-by-step plan the doctor can see.

## 5. Don't forget investors / developers (and land JVs)
- When your usual referral wells feel tapped, ask your **contractors** "who do you know with money that wants to buy/build a building?" Sam's story: a stalled condo project (developer ran out of money, no certificate of occupancy, two deals died) → he asked a contractor → **developer under contract in two days, $2.5M all-cash, 30-day close**; nine months later Sam signed the first lease in the building. Contractors know the money guys (his example: the ski-trip crowd who "own 40 Little Caesars in South America").
- **Land is often too expensive for a solo doctor to build on** (Utah: a good acre under $1M is gone), so bring **developers in for JV partnerships**. Developers who know you **call before they list** ("what do you think of a building here?") → early/first-lease access even though you don't list.

## 6. Network with listing agents — for early & off-market info
Take listing agents to lunch; it pays off with information before it's public. Sam's examples: a building his clients wanted to buy came to him **before it listed** (owner pivoted lease→sale); an agent tipped him to a **corporate dental group going under, ~$40k behind on rent** (a turnkey space). Rules: **always call, never just email**; open with *"I'm a tenant/buyer-only agent, I never list — here are my clients."* Then **pull the listing agents into your network** too (introduce them to your banker/contractors/CPAs) so they call you with second-gen space and early deals.

## 7. Manage the political dynamics between competing vendors
There's **always** a political layer because there's always competition (classically **Schein vs. Patterson vs. Benco** — very competitive, they take it personally, they want to be *in* every deal).
- **The all-inclusive framing:** on any deal, tell the vendor *"this is all-inclusive — we're not going anywhere else on this, we're working it together."* Build the trust; over time vendors get "trained" to know CARR works with all suppliers, so it stops being a surprise. **(This reinforces the Schein↔Patterson firewall already in `vendor-relationship-craft.md` — never cross-pollinate a competitor into a deal that originated with the other.)**
- **The Benco confrontation (West Michigan):** Benco saw Instagram deals tied to Patterson, summoned the agents to a table of six reps — "we see these deals, we're not in them, what do we do?" The agent flipped it: *"those suppliers brought them to the table; I need more from YOUR reps — let's drum deals up together and celebrate together."* Aha: don't passively wait for a supplier to refer — work collaboratively, and don't let a quiet supplier feel entitled to deals they didn't source.

## 8. The "bring it to me first and I'll protect you" pact
a senior CARR agent's line that cemented the Alabama equipment rep: **"Bring the deal to me first, and I'll protect you."** The rep started telling Mike about every startup/grand-opening he heard of *before* anyone else; on a **Talladega** deal, a competitor (Atlanta Dental) was also courting the doctor, but because the rep brought it to Mike first, Mike **pushed the rep in and protected him**. Lesson: explicitly offer your best reps protection in exchange for first look — it builds a loyalty loop that beats a scramble. (Ties to `vendor-intro-timeline.md` reciprocity logic.)

## 9. Target the CONSULTATIVE territory reps (and how to find them)
Meet the **equipment rep** (the manager) and ask: *"Who are your most consultative territory reps?"* — the ones **diagnosing** the practice (profitability, growth plans, challenges), not just selling off a product menu. **Menu reps won't refer deals** — they're not asking the questions that surface a real-estate need, and you usually can't teach it. Over a long territory tenure you learn who they are (the Alabama panelist noted every rep in a market eventually knows who the consultative, deal-making reps are).
- **Co-travel with hungry young reps:** get in the car on their route — doctors say "oh, a real-estate guy, let me talk to you," and reps are shocked how many new conversations happen. Tell them *"I don't want your milk run — let's go chase the accounts you're *trying* to win."*

## 10. Why equipment reps SHOULD refer you (the incentive to name out loud)
A relocation / ground-up / big project almost always means the doctor **buys new equipment to match the new space** → a **commission for the territory rep.** The Alabama rep's first-ever CARR exposure: a doctor got a **TI allowance** (which the rep had never heard of), used it to buy **another room of equipment** → "cha-ching, $40k more equipment sold." So the pitch to a rep is concrete: *our work grows your equipment sale* — it's a long game, but when they kick you deals and you do your job, they win. (Extends the "helps them sell more" pitch in `vendor-relationship-craft.md`.)

## 11. the mapping team's giver mindset — make it about THEM
- **Flip the vendor meeting:** go in asking *"What are your biggest challenges? What are you seeing in the market? Who do you need to know?"* — and *"Who should I know in your network?"* Align on core values, goals, and vision, so the relationship is deeper than deal-swapping.
- **Close the referral loop graciously:** when a vendor emails an intro, thank them for entrusting their client, **and reinforce the vendor to the client** ("you're in great hands with ___") — you strengthen *their* relationship, not just yours. Then keep the vendor in the loop on the deal's progress (don't take the lead and go silent).
- **Why it works:** *"So many people enter our lives with their hands out. When you genuinely want to help someone succeed, they're not hearing that from other real-estate people."* That's the win-win stickiness — "why would I work with anyone else?"

## 12. Practice brokers as networking partners
the mapping team: a practice broker connected her to a Schein territory rep she didn't know → a renewal that became **her largest commission to date.** Practice brokers do the *practice* sale but not leases, so they want to be **connectors** (look good, help their supplier teammates). On **acquisition** deals be careful — they're delicate; don't be a "bull in a china shop." Positioning line: *"I'm here to take the emotion out of it"* — the impartial third party who moves the deal (most acquisition deals **die at the real-estate side**, so you're there to *support the deal*, fast, not just represent your buyer). A GC with an inside track can likewise **rescue** a deal (the mapping team's vet-parking/pet-relief story — the GC solved a parking problem the architect couldn't and sold it to the landlord). Ties to the "way into practice brokers" tactic in `DNA/Leads/pipeline-craft.md` / the 2026 panel.
