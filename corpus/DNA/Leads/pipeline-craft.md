# Part A — Producer habits & channels (the doctrine)

*Distilled from CARR's Producers Podcast episodes, the 2026 National Meeting top-producer panel, and the "How Top Agents Stay Efficient" panel (source sessions identified in the Source Material ingestion ledger; paraphrase-only, producers anonymized per the Jul 8 policy). INTERNAL agent-development material, not prospect-facing. Where a habit is profile-specific it's tagged: **the rapid-build producer** (NYC, ex-Wall Street, top production in ~18 months), **the renewal-specialist producer** (Columbus, ex-practice-lending banker, ~250 transactions in 7 years, 40–50 deals/yr).*

## 1. The math & the ramp

- **You don't need market share — you need a sliver** (the founder's framing). Every metro has "a couple thousand deals a year" (Denver Metro alone: ~250 dental deals/yr). Capturing **1% — even ⅓ of 1%** — makes as much money as you want: two deals/mo ≈ **$400k/yr**; some markets clear **$500k+** at that pace. "I have a competitor in my market" is a non-issue at that math.
- **The rocket-launch principle.** It takes the most fuel to get off the ground: invest heavy hours the first 6–12 months and it compounds — in ~5 years it's your business at 35–45 hrs/wk with control. The founder's companion image: 6–12 months pushing a boulder uphill, then lateral, then it crests and the momentum is hard to stop. **You can't bake the cake faster than it rises.**
- **The 200-vendor front-load** — the same game plan in every market: meet **~150–200 vendors in the first 4–6 months** (the founder did his in 90–120 days: ~30–40 business bankers, ~10 attorneys, ~5 marketing companies, etc.). Out of that falls your A/B/C referral bench, and **4–6 months of front-loaded networking sets you up for 15–20 years** — after which networking stops being "coffee meetings" and becomes talking deal status in the car.
- **Drive is personal.** For one top producer it was a big W2 going to zero on day one plus a taste for the thrill of winning business. Find yours — it's the fuel for the front-load.

## 2. Sourcing channels (ranked by what actually produces)

### The referral bench — A/B/C structure
- The universal top-agent answer to "where do deals come from": **3–4 "A" sources** (heavy hitters — CPAs, lenders, consultants, societies), **~15 "B" sources** (1–2 deals/yr each, but they add up), plus C's you keep developing. Bat **.100 (1 for 10) → you need ~20 referral sources**; bat **.200 → you're begging for an associate.** Internal CARR referrals eventually become the #1 growth channel.
- **Don't be "owned" by one vendor.** A diversified referral base beats depending on one or two sources — referrals should come from all over, not one lender.

### Associations & societies — the single highest-ROI channel
- Reinforces `lead-system.md` line 69: association/society meetings out-produce everything else per hour invested. The founder's **60–90-second pitch** beats a 30-minute PowerPoint: who you are + what you do + **one net-worth testimonial** ("this doctor's building will be worth more than their practice in 10–15 years — anyone want an asset worth more than your practice?") → off the stage, card on every table, walk to the back → 2–4 doctors follow you = a **$50–150k night in two minutes.** These rooms exist in every market (17–20 in Denver alone, reachable with one phone call).
- **Ethnic/cultural medical associations are goldmines** — one producer landed **3 dental/specialist-MD deals** from a Vietnamese Medical Association chapter (a client on its board introduced him around). Also: clients who invite you to society dinners; **booth/15-second plugs** at vertical events (one plastic-surgery event: 6 leads, 2–3 likely transactions); past clients with big social followings plugging you.
- **Public speaking is a trainable muscle** — producers join Toastmasters or take a speaking class to get in front of more societies. Spare research time → Google local speaking events + associations ("30–40 docs who meet monthly — I can tap in there").

### Your existing network — "everybody is a client"
- Your entire pre-CARR network — school, college, every relationship you brought with you — is prospect territory, not just the CARR-taught vendor bench. People who already know you can **vouch for you at a depth a new contact can't.** Most agents under-work this.
- **Just tell people what you do — the 10-hour deal.** The rapid-build producer told a fellow private-school parent "I'm new in this, here's what I do"; she knew a doctor needing space; deal lined up in ~**10 hours** — his best of the year. The whole cause was saying it out loud to his existing orbit. (Another agent got deal intros from his **barber** — you never know who knows people.)
- **Mine a warm connector's LinkedIn.** "Go on my LinkedIn, find 10 people you think are a fit" — expand off people who already know you, not cold.

### The renewals lane — the underweighted channel
- The renewal-specialist producer leaned into renewals from day one — talked about them to supply reps and contractors more than startups/relocations; his **first deal was a renewal**, and renewals are still **~25% of his book**. Renewals are the most common transaction and the most-often-unrepresented — a real opening for a pipeline-builder. Talk renewals to your vendor bench so they refer them. *(Process, posture, and objection scripts live in `DNA/Deal Management/playbooks/renewals.md`; the call craft: the **first two minutes of the first landlord call** is the most critical part — prepare relentlessly, know the top 5–7 objections cold, and always phone over email.)*

### Unusual sources worth knowing
- Dental **temp-staffing agencies** (they know every dentist in town and their volume); **fractional CFOs**; **insurance brokers**; **practice brokers & attorneys** (see the free-comps play in §3); **CARR-family referrals** — serve co-workers' small deals at a high level and they bring you the big ones, plus your vendor bench travels to their clients. *(National accounts are deliberately NOT listed here — they're a separate business model, not a territory sourcing channel; see Part C.)*

### Multi-location groups with an associate bench (added 2026-07-29)
- **The signal:** a group that seats **one equity-holding dentist per location** and carries associates two-plus years out has a retention problem that only new locations solve. Those associates want ownership; the ambitious ones leave to get it. So the group must keep opening — **in the towns those associates want to live in**, which in our territory means the coast. Their expansion demand is structural and recurring, not opportunistic, which makes them a repeat client rather than a transaction.
- **How to qualify one fast:** how many dentists total, how many are under three years out of school, and how many of those hold equity. A group with a third of its doctors on the bench with no equity path is going to be building or buying within a year whether or not they've said so out loud.
- **Where to find them:** state and regional group-practice rosters, the DSO trade press, and the industry conferences (Dykema is the big one). Vendor bench works here too — supply reps, labs and dental lenders know which groups are hunting.
- **Read the gap between public and private strategy.** A group's press releases describe the strategy it has already committed to. What it tells you privately is where it's heading next, and that's the lane where it has the least experience and the most need for us. Our C-156 client publicly announces "three to five established practice acquisitions a year" and has never mentioned ground-up construction anywhere; privately they've stood up a separately capitalized entity to build roughly three from scratch annually and asked us where to put them.
- **Not the same as a national account.** A regional group transacting in our own territory is an ordinary representation client (Parts A–B). Part C applies only to genuine multi-market national accounts.

### Distressed-resale radar — the consolidation hangover (added 2026-07-29)
- **The thesis** (an informed group operator's read, not verified): PE roll-ups bought and combined practices, then rates rose and they couldn't flip to the next buyer, so they're holding underperformers. Expected next phase is lenders pulling back on weak locations and selling them off, since banks want to be in neither the dental nor the real estate business. That produces **resales of existing, built-out practices**.
- **The tell to watch for:** group-owned locations going **dark for months** before re-tenanting. We've observed this locally already.
- **Why it's a sourcing channel:** a built-out healthcare box coming back to market is two openings at once — a buyer client who'd rather acquire than start up, and a landlord who suddenly needs a qualified replacement tenant and has every reason to take our call. Pairs with the renewals lane and with the CoStar Alerts habit in `DNA/Research/costar-how-to.md`.
- *(Vertical detail in `DNA/Reference/dental-vertical-guide.md` — group buy box and market cycle note.)*

### Do small deals with excellence
- **Never turn your nose up at a small deal.** Agents who say "that 1,600 SF / 5-yr deal is too small" **don't get the call on the 8,000 SF one.** Do the small deal with excellence → you get the big one. (Office space especially: lots of inventory, landlords pay 5%, and the landlord-side commission is often negotiable *up* because they'll sacrifice to generate momentum.)

### Regulatory-change radar
- **A state rule change can create a wave of ownership deals before competitors notice.** Example: North Carolina's CON (Certificate of Need) change (Nov 2024) let ambulatory surgical centers open without a CON — a brand-new ownership opportunity for surgeons (an ASC OR builds out at ~$400–600/SF, so redeveloping an existing office building beats ground-up). Being on the forefront of a regulatory change is a differentiator and a referral magnet. **For Joe: track AL/FL CON and scope-of-practice changes.**

## 3. Listing brokers, landlords & competitors (the relational-equity cluster)

- **Treat competitors and listing agents as colleagues, with respect.** You want every competitor (tenant-only shops AND big-firm healthcare groups) to know and respect you — big firms let deals fall through the cracks, and a landlord rep who's seen you across the table on a couple of medical deals will want to work with you. You can negotiate hard AND be liked.
- **Show every client multiple spaces** — not because they'll pick five, but so five different listing brokers/landlords learn you have active clients. **Credibility in a market = doing deals + showing space.** Make the big listing agents show you space personally (not their assistant) when you have a real requirement.
- **Negotiate on 3–4 properties every time — for the relational equity** (the founder's discipline), not because the client needs four. Goal: your number **programmed into the listing broker's phone**, and the "who are you" conversation killed with as many brokers as possible before you need them.
- **Call the landlord to THANK them and COMPLIMENT their broker after a deal** — even an average broker. Nobody else does this; landlords only ever get "will you list this?" calls. Result: **landlords start calling you directly** on future deals — "hundreds of conversations with landlords who *have* listing brokers but go straight to me."
- **Demonstrate competence in your first email to a new listing broker:** reference the specific zoning, condo-doc restrictions, PD setbacks — so in a **feeding frenzy on a property**, an agent who barely knows you still advocates for your client ("their offer may not be the best, but I know *this* one closes"). One producer won 2–3 deals a year purely on that edge; email for discovery also keeps a written record.
- **Bring national-account clients to listing brokers** — when they see you deliver tenants they could never source, they bring you off-market opportunities; reciprocate by matching their listings to other CARR agents' clients.
- **Do unpaid favors as an investment:** run comps for an attorney mid-acquisition (20 min, no fee); check in on clients *during* construction even when there's nothing to fix.
- **The "free comps" way into practice brokers & attorneys** (the founder's play): *"if you ever need comps, just call — don't even tell me who the client is — I'll pull them or give an unofficial BOV."* A half-dozen favors a year returned **15–20 referrals/yr.** The other way in: **offer to take over the third-party-landlord negotiation** they legally can't do (conflict representing both sides).

## 4. Client communication, expectations & boundaries

- **Over-communicate — the weekly "no-update update."** Update every doctor every week, even with nothing new: "Happy Monday — no update, just wanted you to know I'm on your project; I checked with the landlord." Reach out *before* they can reach you. Every inbound → "who can I update with this?" — then set the next action 3–5 business days out. **Move every deal forward one inch a day**, on all 40 at once.
- **"We're a team now."** At engagement, re-read the intake notes, then set expectations: "First I research the market — 2–3 weeks depending on how fast listing brokers respond" (sets the stage *and* buys time when you're slammed). Enlist the client: *"If you see a space I might miss — snap a photo on your drive home and send it."* Prevents the "you didn't find these, I'm losing faith" erosion and earns grace if you slip.
- **Know the client's WHY and mirror it back during the hard stretch:** "Five months ago you said you *had* to own your practice and your real estate — we're in the tough part now, but in a month this is done and that's exactly what we set out to do." **Encourage them**, especially when they're falling apart. What clients say at the finish is "you did a great job AND you were fun to work with" — the fun matters because every deal hits deal-fatigue.
- **Relate to the cold-feet client:** be gracious, not panicked ("I totally understand, it's a big decision"), then reframe — *"your default rate on this is ~1%; I took a bigger gamble coming into a zero-salary career."* Same level → they'll hear your "here's why I think you should move forward."
- **Kill the texting habit with doctors** (a strong, unanimous boundary): text only with close vendors. Text a *doctor* and they'll text you at 6–8 p.m. and expect all-hours access. Reply to a doctor's text with "please email me" + your address. **"Commercial real estate is almost never urgent"** — a doctor who texts today might get a reply tomorrow; that teaches them. Tell clients up front there's no urgency; they back off and often apologize. **Deal points by phone/email, never text** — nothing legally meaningful over text; text is fine for quick follow-ups and sharing contacts.
- **Blame the third party to reshape a tour time:** doctor demands "Thursday 4 p.m."? "The listing broker won't let us in after 5" — one soft push-back and the doctor finds Tuesday 9 a.m. Don't over-cater out of fear of losing the deal. **Set the tour expectation:** agents show 8–5 weekdays, so the doctor carves out 2–4 hours; tours run in geographic order; paper tour packet in hand.

## 5. Deal-making craft

- **Put a contractor/architect on your side of the table.** Someone who won't trash an imperfect space and instead reframes constraints — "we fit 4 ops not 5, plumbing costs more, but here's how we handle it." A **non-broker in the room saying "this is pretty good"** de-risks it for the client and pushes the deal.
- **Sell the deal to the LANDLORD, not just the client.** Include in-depth **bios** in proposals — especially for younger doctors with thin financials: where they trained, why dental is a great business, and that **dentists stay 40+ years after an expensive build-out** (low turnover = landlord security).
- **Get creative on structure** in high-rent markets: restructure the standard 3% escalation, use key money / out-of-pocket, or shift rate structures later in the lease. Everything's negotiable once the landlord is open to the use and understands the client's constraints.
- **Talk monthly payment, not price** (the renewal-specialist producer's #1 hack from his banking days): "it's not a $700k building, it's a ~$4,200/mo payment." Cash flow is king; run the numbers with the doctor in the passenger seat. *(Also a content-bank entry — `DNA/Marketing/Social Media/content-inspiration-bank.md` §2, routed Jul 7, 2026.)*
- **Frame TI as the landlord's investment against the net deal value:** the landlord sees the net of all payments over the term as one chunk and will invest TI + commissions + legal to capture it. Rough bands: **retail TI ≈ 0–20%** of deal value, **office ≈ 15–45%** (if desperate) — or express it as the landlord's **break-even year** (year 3 on a 10-yr). On triple-net, strip out op-ex — pass-throughs aren't profit. **Ask the listing agent "what's important to THIS landlord?"** — it differs every time (ties to `DNA/Deal Management/playbooks/negotiation.md`).
- **Don't over-ask on TI** *(content angle, routed Jul 7, 2026, Joe-approved)*: asking $50–60/SF on every deal backfires — **if the client doesn't need it, extra TI just gets baked back into the rent.** Know their lending ability + construction budget and ask for what they actually need. → doctor-facing angle in `content-inspiration-bank.md` §2; the rest of this TI section is internal.
- **Asking rate on tours — two valid schools:** keep it vague ("$25/SF" sets a false anchor; concessions drive the *effective* rate — "let's see what passes the eye test, then worry about economics") **or** print the asking monthly rate on the tour packet labeled "asking, not yet negotiated" so the doctor can calibrate. Pick your style.
- **Never leave a key right out of the LOI** (an expensive lesson): a relocation & termination right omitted from an LOI surfaced at lease signing and **the deal died.** Get critical rights into the LOI, not "we'll add it later." *(The flip side of the legal session's "punt the sticking point to the lease" tactic — punt a negotiable point, never drop a right you need.)*

> **Cross-ref — office-over-retail (routed Jul 7, 2026, Joe-approved both ways):** the argument for steering practices from retail into office space — *pays roughly half the rent, better window lines / natural light, avoids competing with Starbucks/Chipotle for the space, and the client can pour the $3.50–4/SF + rent savings into targeted marketing they can turn on and off.* Lives as **(a)** a full space-type advisory in `DNA/Deal Management/playbooks/diligence-and-valuation.md`, and **(b)** a doctor-facing content angle in `DNA/Marketing/Social Media/content-inspiration-bank.md` §2. Caveat carried through both: **veterinary, Aspen-Dental-type brands, and some retail-dependent uses still need retail** — it's "9 of 10 tenants," not all. (The commission upside is real but kept INTERNAL — never the client pitch.)

## 6. Organization & efficiency

**Run the job — don't let the schedule run you** (the through-line of every efficiency discussion). The systems below exist to survive the chaos.

### The week
- **Keep a set Office Day** (the founder has preached this for 12+ yrs). Monday works — you can't get organized *during* the week. **Be true to it**: don't become a slave to vendor meetings/tours and skip it — the office day is where **follow-ups** happen.
- **Meeting-driven weeks vs. block scheduling — both work.** Set meetings 1–2 weeks out and fill the holes with office work, or reserve blocks ("Tuesday = cold calls"). Pick the one that fits your brain.
- **Protect your mornings** ("my mornings are my mornings; I don't do early tours" — the tone attracts clients who align with it). **Do calls during the commute** — a 60–90 min drive is call time. **Prioritize by client, not just geography:** the 6,000 SF client tours before the 1,200 SF one; best attention to the most serious, responsive, ready-to-move clients. Triage your bandwidth honestly.

### The day
- **Morning lists + hardest thing first.** Up early, write down everything for the day, do the hardest work first — the tough call you procrastinate "eats you alive all day"; clearing it frees you to fly through the rest. **No procrastination, period.**
- **Inbox at or near zero, folders non-negotiable.** The renewal-specialist producer had three emails in his inbox at recording — constantly filing into *Doctor* / *Closed doctors* / *Future* folders. A weekly variant: the **Sunday inbox reset** — sort by person, sweep 40 emails into deal folders at once. An inbox under control is the tell of a producer who controls the work instead of the work controlling him.
- **Physical checklist by the computer** — draw boxes, check them off (check a land parcel, call the signage contractor, create an LOI). Emails are a separate task set.
- **Manage distraction — two valid, opposite styles:** (a) **phone OFF for focus blocks** — a CoStar search that took an hour while multitasking is really a 15-minute job; "they'll just call back"; or (b) **the one-touch philosophy** — if a call comes in and you can take it, take it and clear it; end the day with a near-empty inbox knowing exactly where you left off. Fast turnaround also builds vendor goodwill (a banker thanked one producer for a 10-second lease send). If multitasking drops your output, switch to single-tasking with the phone off. **Important vs. urgent** (the *7 Habits* frame): don't let the *urgent* run your life.

### Lead intake & CRM (one concrete, battle-tested system)
- **New lead → one sheet of paper first:** doctor's name, source, everything known pre-call → make the intro call → ideal ending is writing **"send ETL"** at the bottom.
- **CRM with scheduled next activities** (this producer uses ACT!): enter the contact, schedule the next action ("send ETL today"), update at day's end, print tomorrow's task list — it can surface a task set 6 months ago, and its history holds 20 years of calls/meetings. "A lot of duplicity, but nothing ever slips through the cracks." (Another panelist's reaction to the printed list: "that gave me anxiety" — again, pick the system that fits your brain.)
- **Folders everywhere, one per doctor:** physical folder (paper notes), matching digital desktop folder (tour packets, LOIs, commission agreements), and an email folder. Repetition makes it stick.

### Property-search workflow (a real time-saver)
- Vet properties in CoStar, then send the **same copy-paste email** to every listing: *"I have a dental client looking for ~2,500 SF — is this a good fit? What's the asking rate + pass-through expenses?"* (One bulleted email per broker with several listings.) Mark **"E / date"** on each, follow up on non-responders in 3–4 days (CC any co-broker). Narrows ~15 → ~6–7. **You can send these from within CoStar** (contacts page → auto-titles with the property name → copies you) — a big time-saver at 20–30 deals.
- **Pre-tour when you can** — showing up to a dump makes the client think you don't know what you're doing. When you can't, use the hedge: "These are qualified on paper — use, parking, accessibility — but I haven't seen inside either, so we're seeing it fresh together."
- **Template emails by problem type:** save canned emails for the recurring issues (assignment clause, etc.) and fire the right one instead of re-writing. The career shifts from *sourcing* deals to *closing them efficiently* — the same problems recur, so systematize the responses.

### Work-life balance (the CARR promise, actually kept)
- **"8 AM at your desk, 5 PM at your desk."** The renewal-specialist producer's whole success formula in one line. In 7 years / 250 transactions: **never a weekend tour**, ~5–7 weekend work calls total, **emails OFF 6 PM Friday → Monday morning** (literally toggled off in settings). "Anything can be managed in the morning — nothing is so big it can't wait 12 hours." CARR leadership means it about not responding in the evenings. Weekend outreach won't get a weekend reply, and it doesn't affect the deal.

## 8. Mindset & first-year advice (the speed round)

- **Trust the playbook / don't reinvent the wheel.** "I've never seen someone follow the game plan and have it not work; I've seen people not follow it and have it not work." Where agents derail: deciding "it doesn't work in my market, I'll do it my way" — e.g. sending an **LOI when the situation calls for an RFP**. Do the first few transactions the specific, coached way → the habits stick → that's how a CARR agent runs 50 deals/yr when other brokers cap at 15–20.
- **Don't over-negotiate — drop the ego.** Once you've gone a couple rounds and gotten the best available deal within the market's window, **get it done and get paid** — "I need to beat the other broker" costs the client.
- **Be a problem-solver with a short memory.** Deals fall apart; "the deal's not dead — it just needs to be put back on track," then find the next one. Don't gauge yourself against 5-year veterans — **compare yourself to who you were yesterday.**
- **Use the resources — ask the "dumb" question.** 150+ people you can call; Agent Central has the answer. Asking a question you already know the answer to makes you act more confidently.
- **Find YOUR growth method** and stop wasting time on ones that don't fit you. Bad at cold calling but great at 10 vendor meetings a week? Lean all the way in. CARR gives the model; adapt it to your strengths.

# Part B — Sourcing in tight-inventory markets
*What to do when you have a lead but CoStar shows one/two/zero options (from a CARR agent-training FAQ session; source in the ingestion ledger). **Especially relevant to Joe** — his territory has genuinely thin submarkets (Dothan/Wiregrass, rural stretches, parts of the Panhandle), so this is a core skill, not an edge case. Complements `DNA/Research/costar-how-to.md` §9 (the CoStar mechanics), Part A §3 (show multiple spaces / get known by listing brokers) & §6 (pre-tour, property-search workflow), and `lead-system.md` (self-sourced prospecting).*

> **The mindset:** you become a *maker of deals out of thin air* — the longer you're in a market, the more deals exist only because of you. Don't get frustrated in year one: it gets easier as listing agents/developers/owners learn who you are (a big reason to always work multiple properties — it builds your name so they call you first with off-market space).

## The tactics (a checklist for a thin market)

1. **The CoStar broadcast-email technique (the #1 tactic).** Run a BROAD CoStar search — no filters, wide radius (25 mi) — then List view → **sort by listing agent** → find the **30–40 agents with the most listings** in the market. Email each **one-to-one right inside CoStar**: *"Good morning — I have a [dental/medical/vet/optometry] client looking for ~2,000 SF in/around [area]. Do you happen to have anything on or off market? Thanks,"* + your contact. This does two things: (a) puts your name out ("I'm here, I do healthcare — nice to meet you"), and (b) **surfaces UNLISTED pockets** — a tenant they want to push out, someone about to vacate, a new development not yet public. Run it ~**every 3–4 weeks** on legitimate needs (don't blast the same agents 4× a week). Over time they call *you* first before listing. Tell your client you're doing this — you're turning over every stone, not just running a search.

2. **Convert lease listings into purchases.** Buyer wants to own but nothing's for sale? Pull the 15–20 *lease* options and email those listing agents: *"I have a buyer only interested in purchasing — would your landlord consider selling, or an option to purchase?"* Often the reply is **"everything's for sale, make an offer"**. Frame it for the client: won't be a screaming deal, but for the *right* building it's worth market (or a touch over) for a 10–20 year home.

3. **Submit backup offers.** On properties already under contract / in due diligence — those fall through often, and the first backup offer in line often gets the building.

4. **Get to know listing agents — pitch sessions & networking.** Listing agents present their developments at "pitch sessions"; a good listing-agent network means you hear about properties before they hit the market. Don't *over*-invest early (you rarely get a deal directly from a listing agent), but it compounds.

5. **Get on broker/developer drip campaigns.** Especially: *"Any previously-built-out healthcare space — 2nd-generation dental/optometry — tell me first; I can move those fast."* A move-in-ready second-gen space is a quick win.

6. **On every tour, ask the agent what else is coming up / new developments.** (A listing agent once had another property she hadn't mentioned — added at the end of the tour, and it turned out to be *the* space the client wanted.) Makes you a market expert.

7. **Set client expectations up front.** This isn't residential / Zillow with seven new options a day. ~30 options meeting the SF criteria, then parking + signage + zoning + neighbor filters narrow it to 4–6. Set that table so they don't expect 40 options on a tour.

8. **Pre-tour by driving — CoStar only shows ~70% of the market.** Turn off your phone (a glance = missed properties, and you'll have to re-drive); you can only watch one side of the street per pass, so drive it both ways. **the renewal-specialist producer's vacant-building trick**: spot a nice vacant standalone with no sign (old restaurant, old Blockbuster) → pull the owner from the **property-tax site → Google the LLC → Secretary of State** → contact them. He's gone under contract on a building that was never listed and had no sign.

9. **Ground-up development — the last resort.** No building or lease options? Look at **land + a developer** who builds and leases it back (some with an option to purchase). Exhaust everything above first — land is the longest process to walk a client through.

10. **Put the client on a drip / CoStar alert and STAY TOP OF MIND.** Daily alert on new matches → call/email the client when one pops. The danger: show two meh options, then go silent 3 weeks — meanwhile they're browsing LoopNet and driving around on weekends, and they'll **call the listing agent themselves and cut you out.** Constant touches ("would you be open to this? what about that?") keep you their guy: *"he's my guy, I'll send it to him."*

## When one firm holds most of the listings in a small market (founder Q&A, Nov 2024)
*(Added July 7, 2026 — from a Nov 2024 founder Q&A; source in the ingestion ledger. Directly relevant to Joe's small AL/FL submarkets.)*
- One firm rarely holds **all** listings — find the other firms, pocket listings, or **create an option** (ground-up / purchase; small markets = cheaper RE, so these are genuinely viable). Same "manufacture posture" logic as the tactics above.
- **The pivotal question: is it the same LISTING BROKER, or the same OWNER?** Just ask the broker directly ("you handle 1st Street and South Broadway — same ownership group or different owners?"). **Different owners changes everything:** you have real posture, can request **direct owner calls**, and can note in your offer that different ownership groups are competing through one broker — which that broker is *required to present*, exposing any steering/conflict. If it's genuinely one owner + one broker, you must get more creative (create an option).
- **Build direct relationships with the big owners** in your submarkets ("your listing broker's doing a great job — I just want you to know who I am; happy to be a sounding board on getting more doctors into your properties"). In small markets you'll also meet **residential agents dabbling in commercial** who don't fully know the game.

## Bonus — converting a restaurant to medical (Q&A)
Common in markets where the only standalone buildings left are restaurants. Big cost items: **cooler removal, the hood, grease traps**, and above all the **facade/exterior** (don't leave it looking like a Taco Bell / Pizza Hut — can be a substantial redo; lean on the GC). Environmentally, a **standard Phase One is usually enough** for a restaurant — the environmental risks are gas stations, automotive shops, and dry cleaners, not restaurants. # Part C — National Accounts (a SEPARATE business model)

*Created 2026-07-11 (portal overview session + the National Accounts Guidelines Apr 2023 and Transacting Guidelines Jan 2023 docs); expanded same day with the full 4-part National Accounts Masterclass (Joe attended the live cohort; sources in the ingestion ledger; Joe scoped the merge here). **Joe's standing directive (Jul 11, 2026): national accounts are a separate business model from the territory business — this part is its own avenue with its own team structure, approval process, and economics. Never fold it into the territory doctrine (Parts A–B), and never treat it as just another sourcing channel.** The two models touch at exactly one point: an inbound national-account requirement can land IN your territory — and even then it runs by national-account rules, not the local A-to-Z process. Per-lesson summary docs live on Agent Central under the National Accounts tab.*

## C1. What qualifies (and the term to never confuse it with)
A **national account** is a CLIENT — an entity that signs ETLs and leases or buys in multiple markets. The working definition is broad: **multiple locations, or immediate plans for them.** "One location, maybe two more in ten years" is not an account; "we're looking at five markets across the state right now" is. It doesn't have to cross state lines — a client transacting on the far side of your own state, outside the market you actually control, should be registered too. A franchisor that directs opportunities can qualify even though franchisees sign the individual ETLs. A **corporate relationship** is a referral source (a vendor to our clients — equipment, lending, legal, accounting, consulting) that never signs an ETL; CARR maintains those for everyone's benefit and assigns their leads by market and experience. The one-question test: **will this prospect sign an ETL and be the tenant/buyer in multiple markets?**

## C2. Register, conflict-check, and comply (BEFORE any work)
- **Conflict-check by email before pursuing any target** — the Director of National Accounts keeps the master account list (confidential, never distributed) and answers "is this already someone's account?" same-day. Agents send lists of 10–12 targets at a time; overlap is rare, but checking first is the norm.
- **Register accounts you're working** — registration is protection: when another agent asks about your account, the Director points them to you instead of letting them dig. It's a tracking mechanism, not oversight; nobody gets involved in your business uninvited.
- The client must be **approved and registered in advance** (via the Director / Salesforce) to access the platform benefits: operating in multiple markets (or immediate expansion plans), willing to sign an ETL, and wanting an account manager. A multi-market client that doesn't want the platform benefits skips approval and is treated like any other client.
- **Every national-account deal is preregistered with compliance@carr.us before work begins** (separate from client approval): client/company name, primary contact name, email, phone, transaction type, and location of the requirement. Compliance replies with the requirements and limitations for that jurisdiction.
- Expect the client to sign **state-specific ETLs and disclosures per requirement**, even with an ETL already on file — set that expectation at engagement. There is deliberately no national agreement anymore (it protected neither side legally); agreements are state-by-state or transaction-by-transaction, cancellable by either side, and carve-outs are fine (see the pitch Q&A in C7).
- Existing relationships are respected both ways: don't work an account that has expressed a preference for another CARR agent, and a client with a current ETL should tell any other agent who contacts them.
- **Salesforce naming:** name the deal after the NATIONAL ACCOUNT (never the contact person); put the location or your own identifier in the transaction ID (accounting will normalize it to city/state at close). This keeps every deal for an account searchable under one name.

## C3. The team structure (what makes this model different)
Every national account runs on a three-role team: the **Director of National Accounts** (salaried, non-commissioned — oversees the platform, co-pitches, supports everyone), the **Account Manager** (the client's primary point of contact, deep on the account's business and real-estate preferences), and the **Local Agent** (licensed in the market where a given requirement sits; brings market expertise and fronts landlords/sellers/listing agents). Corporate adds specialist demographics/mapping and lease-administration support. Any agent can hold the Account Manager seat — nobody at CARR is limited to or excluded from national accounts.

## C4. The Local Agent seat (inbound deals — the entry point for new agents)
Account managers deliberately bring deals to NEW agents in the requirement's market — it gets people income and momentum right after training, and a dedicated local agent for national-account work can build a six-figure lane from it. When one calls with a deal in your market:
- It's a **team deal**, not your usual A-to-Z: agree on roles AND the commission split up front, **in writing**, before work starts.
- **Keep the account manager informed at all times** — the client calls them first, and they need to speak to deal status cold. Ask them why the client does things their way instead of guessing; they know the account's deal preferences and deal-killers.
- **NEVER go around the account manager** — not to the client, not to the franchisor, not with a vendor introduction. If the client could use a vendor intro, clear it with the account manager first (same ethic as a banker-sourced deal). Franchisors hire CARR precisely so they don't get calls from twenty different local agents.
- **Follow the CARR game plan exactly** — negotiate multiple properties, use the comparison spreadsheets. Most accounts chose CARR because they saw that process; cutting corners locally can jeopardize the entire account.
- **Brand standards are HARD rules.** A franchise minimum of 1,800 SF has no wiggle room; a 10-foot minimum room dimension exists because the furniture package doesn't fit otherwise. Local-deal habits (pad the CoStar range, "this works perfectly, trust me") kill franchise deals at lease signing. Ask for the standards, apply them literally, ask the account manager when unsure.
- **Gratitude and proportion:** the relationship behind the deal took the account manager months or years to build, and the account may be a large share of their annual income. Handle it well and more come — the agents who run national accounts all talk to each other, and one botched local deal closes the whole lane; a well-run one makes you the market's default.
- The account manager should hand you a **detail report** on the account (client background + the specific requirement) — ask for it if it doesn't arrive. Expect account-level tracking tools too (a Monday.com board or shared tracking sheet you update).
- Introduce yourself to the agents who do heavy national-account volume BEFORE a deal exists — a short email that you're interested in local-agent work is how you get on their call list.

## C5. How the deals themselves differ from local deals
- **Centralized decision-making — arm your contact.** You often won't have access to the real decision layer (a COO, CFO, or PE board). Your point of contact has to re-present your reasoning without you, so put the WHY of every recommendation in writing for them. Deals die at levels you never meet because the contact couldn't answer a question.
- **No personal guarantees, ever** (a corporate officer won't sign one on a million-dollar lease) — with ONE exception: individual franchisees do sign them. Alternatives to have ready: corporate guarantee (usually fine), letter of credit, increased security deposit. Surface the guarantee position up front or the landlord smells bait-and-switch later.
- **Financials positions vary hard:** share-anything · P&L/balance-sheet-under-NDA · absolutely-nothing. For the nothing-group, the listing-agent argument is the track record itself ("hundreds of locations operating successfully"). Know the position before the first LOI; if it kills the deal, better sooner.
- **Standardized LOI/lease requirements.** Seasoned accounts have hardened non-negotiables (like a REIT's 80-page lease — every clause is a scar). Build the account's deal-killers INTO a standardized LOI/RFP template for that account rather than using the stock CARR template, so nothing gets missed and every local agent inherits it.
- **Turnkey — pin down which meaning, then do the math.** Turnkey means either (a) second-generation space needing paint and floors, or (b) the landlord takes on the financial responsibility and project management of the build-out. Most accounts mean (b), because they don't want to manage construction from two states away. Two rules: don't believe "turnkey doesn't happen in my market" (it changes when a proven multi-location tenant asks), and price it honestly — a heavy build-out comes back as an elevated lease rate. Rule of thumb: **$10/SF of TI ≈ +$1/SF/yr of rent on a 10-year term.** Reverse-engineer it and set the expectation with BOTH the client and the listing agent (spring it late and your LOI lands in the trash).
- **The rate-vs-TI spectrum.** Local deals want lowest rate + highest TI. National accounts often want one pole: full turnkey at whatever rate (capital stays in the business; rent is a write-off) or zero TI at the rock-bottom rate (they self-build). Ask which mind they're of at onboarding — transacting the local-deal way for the wrong pole blows deals invisibly.
- **The TI sanity check:** total TI as a share of the landlord's base-rent earnings over the term (strip out CAM/pass-throughs). ~20% is a great deal in a tenant's market (12–15% is common when landlords have the leverage); an ask that pencils to 40% goes in the trash unsent.
- **Term strategy — the flexibility conversation.** Short-term demands ("we only do 3s/5s") have two real reasons: fear of a failing location, or a planned sale of the company. Both have better answers than a short lease: for failure risk, get their actual numbers (20 locations, 2 closed = 90% stay rate) and structure a 7–10 year term WITH a termination right at 3/5/7 (payback = the unamortized TI + commissions — which is all they'd forfeit on a short lease anyway, while banking 10-year-term concessions). For the sale scenario: a buyer would much rather assume good long leases than renegotiate everything at closing — ask for assignment WITHOUT landlord consent when the assignee is equal-or-stronger. If a board caps term anyway, run with it — or park the account (below).
- **Park the too-small-term account rather than working it.** An account that will only sign 1–3 year leases gets no landlord leverage, so an agent adds no value (space-finding is the easy part; negotiating is the value). Stay warm cheaply: send them CoStar lists for markets they name, keep the relationship, and assign it properly when they mature into longer terms.
- **Competition reads differently:** national groups often WANT to be next to competitors (they know the competitor's weaknesses and intend to take share and labor). Ask instead of assuming.
- **Group leverage is real:** 10–15 successful locations should command better concessions than any single doctor — make sure the listing agent and landlord KNOW it's a proven group.
- **Mapping & demographics is the platform's heavy artillery here** (local doctors know their own town; a Chicago group opening in Tulsa knows nothing): population/income/education through competitor and referral-source mapping to the insurance-carrier mix inside a radius. The discipline: **we provide data, never interpretation** — what works for a cosmetic-dentistry group would sink a Medicaid-focused DSO, so site judgment stays with the client (paid third-party interpreters exist to refer if they insist). *(The corporate deliverable is a Sites USA report: a Custom Medical Insurance carrier-mix-by-radius table plus an Expanded Profile of population, income, net worth, daytime demographics, and age; structure in `national-accounts-toolkit.md` section 7.)*
- **PE-backed groups:** involvement runs from "here's $100M, tell us how it went" to "I'm your new CEO." Find out early which one it is — it decides who you're really pitching and whose questions your contact must survive. Not always obvious from branding: many PE groups deliberately keep acquired practices under the local doctor's name.

## C6. Finding your own account (prospecting — deliberately NOT dialing-for-dollars)
Don't abandon territory work to hunt national accounts full-time; fold the hunt into what you already do (vendor meetings, cold calls on anything that smells multi-site) plus a focused weekly block. **Pick a vertical to specialize in** (dental, vet, med spa, autism, franchising…) — the opportunity pool is bottomless and unfocused searching is wheel-spinning. A working target list of ~5 at a time keeps it honest. The toolkit:
- **Franchise channels:** the Entrepreneur Franchise 500, Franchise Times, Franchise Gator — subscribe to the newsletters, filter their directories to health & wellness, then follow the brands and their leadership on LinkedIn.
- **LinkedIn company search:** search the vertical → filter to Companies → location (US or your region) → company size 11–50+ (filters out single-location shops). The right person: small group = CEO/CFO/founder; bigger = operations or real-estate director; anything with "real estate" in the title. The **wrong-person trick:** deliberately message someone adjacent (marketing director), ask who handles real estate, then open with the referrer's name — a self-made warm intro.
- **Apollo.io** (free tier, sign up with a PERSONAL email — company emails hit a paid-seat cap): a LinkedIn extension that surfaces work emails ~80–85% of the time. The Director's paid seat can pull direct phone numbers on request.
- **Google Alerts** (needs a Gmail): standing alerts like "expanding med spa franchise," "emerging DSO," "corporate healthcare expansion" — free inbound prospect flow, quality varies, skim and chase.
- **Private equity portfolios:** search "healthcare private equity in [nearest big metro]." On the firm's site: Team page → target **partner / operating partner / principal** titles (never marketing, analysts, or investor relations); Portfolio page → **active portfolio only** (realized/exited = no relationship left); the team member listed on a portfolio company usually covers several — research all of them before reaching out. AI triage works well: feed the portfolio list to your assistant with CARR's client profile and have it rank fit. If a PE firm is within driving distance, work it like a vendor relationship — face to face.
- **CoStar tenant-locations search:** filter medical space use, ~1,500–50,000 SF occupied (excludes hospitals), company growth = growing/rapidly-growing/stable. The location detail pages list **decision-maker contacts** with titles, location counts, and signed lease terms (a group signing only 3-year terms may not be worth chasing — but verify, CoStar data is only as good as its inputs).
- **Target EMERGING groups above all.** A group already running 200 locations has systems (and every competitor chasing it); you only displace a working system when something breaks, and you can't time that. A group just starting to scale has no system yet — do the work well and you BECOME their real-estate function, and grow as they grow. Smaller start, far stickier, far less competition.

## C7. Outreach and the pitch
**The contact sequence** (roughly 7–10 days, then release):
1. **Research first** — follow the company and the person on LinkedIn, Google for press releases, have AI dig for real-estate pain points (how they transact today, existing broker partnerships).
2. **Email #1:** subject line = something you learned about THEM ("congrats on the Fort Worth opening"), not "real estate." Body ≤5 sentences: the thing you noticed, one line on who CARR is, a soft CTA for a **15-minute** call (ask for 30–45 and you won't get it).
3. **Email #2 at 3–5 days** (longer and it reads as a brand-new email): same shape, rotate the value angle (renewals, why buyer/tenant-only matters).
4. **Phone call:** they won't answer — that's fine, the scripted voicemail IS the play (proves you're human) + "I'll send one more email in case they've gone to spam."
5. **LinkedIn message:** even briefer; read-receipts help. Check for genuinely-known mutual connections.
6. **The breakup email:** "maybe now isn't the time; keep my info." Regularly triggers the meeting, because busy people act when the reminders are about to stop.
7. **Fresh angle up the chain:** approach the CEO/CFO as a NEW outreach (never "your person didn't respond") — profitability is their vested interest and they forward it down with instructions to book you.
- Optional personal layer: short embedded video in the email (Vidyard-type tools; avatar generation included) — a face with the name, and it separates you from bot spam.

**The pitch call itself** — bring the Director in for your first 2–4 (salaried, takes nothing off your table; positions as "high-level platform resource" and pitches YOU as the account manager; prefers Zoom to demo live). The structure:
1. You open + introductions, then hand off (or run it yourself when seasoned): **"Tell me about the company, your role, and how you're handling real estate today"** — then let them talk. If pain points don't surface on their own, ask directly. Everything after gets aimed at what they name.
2. CARR overview: the two niches — healthcare-only, and (even more important) **buyer/tenant-only, never assumed to be understood** — explain the conflict-of-interest logic unless they're a career real-estate person.
3. **The four service categories,** offered à la carte (some clients use one, some all four, never a fee either way — and CARR has eaten fees when landlords went bankrupt rather than bill a client): **facilitating growth** (de novo, relocation, expansion, acquisitions' real-estate component; the only can't-do is listing what they need to sell or sublease) · **providing critical data** (mapping/demographics per C5, including insurance mapping — "which carriers do we need in-network BEFORE the doors open") · **ensuring protection** (send us every lease; a third-party service abstracts each to ~a page for the practice manager's fingertips; renewal ticklers fire 12–18 months out; renewals then run the SAME multi-property negotiation process, because a landlord who knows you can't move gives you nothing) · **access to industry partners** (vetted investors/developers: build-to-suit-then-lease when inventory fails, or sale-leaseback to free trapped capital when banks tap out and PE dilution is unwanted).
4. **Show the comparison spreadsheet — but call it the "financial analysis"** with C-suite people. Walk the five-address logic: deals die (pivot instantly), landlord-vs-landlord leverage (savvy landlords smell a no-alternatives tenant), and the client owns their numbers at decision time. Show how it grows columns with each counter round.
5. Platform efficiency proof point: a single account manager has run 200+ deals for one client in a year — possible only because the account manager quarterbacks while local agents carry local execution ("best of both worlds": one point of contact who knows the business + boots-on-the-ground market knowledge in every city).
6. **The agreement Q&A** (know these cold): no national agreement, state-by-state/per-transaction instead; cancellable by either side any time, never contested; **carve-outs are fine** ("keep your Oklahoma partner — once you see what we do you'll want us everywhere, but we'll earn it"); non-exclusive agency agreements exist if they push. The ONE hard line: **never two firms working the same transaction** — it tells landlords the tenant is disorganized and inflates the landlord's read of their own property's value.
7. Close: "what's next" = copies of the deck + the National Accounts Profile (Agent Central) for the board, lease copies if they want abstracting, and the **onboarding call**.

## C8. Running the account (the Account Manager seat)
**The onboarding call** = the local intake ritual plus the national-account layer: their position on **term** (educate on term value and the termination-clause structure per C5), **rate-vs-TI pole**, **guarantees**, **financials sharing**, **PE involvement**, and **communication cadence** (let THEM set it: weekly/biweekly/monthly calls; reporting depth from "ping me at LOI" to "photos of every site"). Goal: you stop needing to ask — you answer landlord questions from knowledge and things stop reaching the client's desk at all. That's when you've become the unpaid extension of their team no one wants to function without.

**Deal roles and splits — two different animals:**
- **Franchise accounts: ~70/30** (local agent / account manager). The franchisee is a NEW decision-maker every deal, so the local agent runs essentially a full local deal; the account manager is the go-between for franchisor ↔ franchisee ↔ local agents — keeps the master tracking board (Monday.com or a shared sheet) current, runs the franchisor cadence call, troubleshoots so the franchisor never fields twenty agents' calls, and polices brand standards.
- **Corporate/PE accounts (same decision-maker every deal): ~50/50** (never drift toward 70/30 here), and the account manager STAYS the primary point of contact. Three reasons: quality control (the client gets the SAME experience every market instead of a different agent's style each time), account learning (delegate the relationship and you never build the knowledge that makes their transacting efficient), and account protection (hand the relationship to local agents and the account may walk out the door with a departing agent). The 20%-referral-style handoff exists and sometimes works — it's playing with fire.
- **The local-agent proposal that makes ~50/50 land:** "I do the initial search and send you the list; you vet it, add your pocket-listing/pre-tour knowledge, tour the client, and send the RFP introducing me as account manager taking over from there (you stay CC'd on everything); I run every negotiation A to Z from that point." Pair it with the standing offer: if the workload turns out heavier than described, come back and we restructure. Splits flex for state legalities or a brand-new local agent needing handholding.
- **Pick local agents deliberately** — ask the heavy national-account agents who they use in that market, or ask the Director. Wrong pick = dead deal and a frustrated account.

**Franchise-specific craft:** the territory-analysis service is a genuine differentiator — inventory + average-lease-rate snapshots for a zip-code set or polygon BEFORE the franchisor sells the territory (franchisors have been sued over territories that turned out to be half national park). Time-stamp every snapshot ("this is today's market, not a promise"). Franchisee group chats set network-wide expectations off one outlier deal ("my lease is $15/SF!") — arming the franchisor with real market data per territory is the antidote, and it makes your own deals easier.

**Tour prep at distance:** build a Google Earth package per market — every tour property pinned with photos, suite/SF, advertised rate, op-ex, the account's specific criteria (outdoor space? turnkey?), and the CoStar brochure link (opening a CoStar brochure leaves CoStar, so the link is shareable). A client flying in can kill half the list from their desk ("saves the trip" is the point — clients call it non-negotiable once they've had it). Manual data entry, but reusable. CAUTION: only include listing-broker contact info once the client is trained never to go around you.

**Tracking:** Salesforce can't run a 15-deal review call. Keep an account-level tracker (spreadsheet or Monday board — the Director has a blank template) so the random Tuesday "what's going on with 123 Main St?" call gets an instant answer.

**The compounding loop:** run one account well and it opens others — clients know their peers in other markets (they won't intro same-market competitors), so ASK: for referrals, for the conferences where their colleagues gather. Communication and detail obsession are the whole game — same as local, just with more people watching.

## C9. For Joe & Dell (early-stage posture toward this model)
The territory business (Parts A–B) is the primary focus — that's also CARR's own guidance (the masterclass itself warns against sitting at a computer dialing for national accounts all day). Joe and Dell's standing rule (restated Jul 11, 2026): national accounts stay a deliberate minority of the pipeline. The model is lucrative but pulls you off the street and into a desk job, and the entire point of going deep locally is to be out in the market. Bonus lane, never the main road. The avenue costs little to keep open: **(1)** the Director of National Accounts knows Joe (live masterclass cohort, named alongside Dell and the Mobile–Tallahassee territory on day one) — keep that visibility warm so inbound Local Agent deals route here; **(2)** introduce yourself by email to the heavy national-account agents as willing local-agent hands in South AL / the Panhandle; **(3)** when a vendor mentions a multi-site or expanding group, conflict-check it with the Director by email, then decide pursue/park; **(4)** if a real target emerges, take the co-pitch offer — first 2–4 pitches with the Director on the call; **(5)** candidate vertical focus if/when Joe hunts deliberately: pick ONE (the territory's DSO/vet/med-spa mix suggests itself, but that's Joe's call, not doctrine).

## Out-of-market economics (general transacting rules, not national-account-specific)
*From the Transacting Guidelines — these apply to ANY out-of-market deal, either business model.* Agents may work any market they're licensed in, client side only. Source a deal outside your licensure and you either **refer it** (recommended ~20% to the sourcing agent; requires confirmed client communication + Salesforce entry + an email intro) or **cooperate** on it with a locally licensed CARR agent (recommended 25–50% to the sourcing agent, splits in 5% increments) — and anything beyond a pure referral needs **advance compliance review** (some jurisdictions bar co-brokerage with out-of-state licensees entirely). A deal is protected once it's in Salesforce with a fully executed, current ETL — enter deals promptly. No repackaging: a deal CARR or another agent hands you can't be re-referred for a fee. Splits live in Salesforce and lock once accounting processes the closing, so confirm ratios with cooperating agents before close-out.

## Signal intelligence from the outbound world (merged 2026-07-31 from the Mallery GTM checklist, x.com/fin465/status/2083259166337196169, read in full — its cold-email VOLUME machinery declined wholesale per the one-gate relationship model; the SIGNALS half transplants cleanly onto the radar)

Three adopted, applying to territory pipeline AND national accounts alike:

1. **The decision window: a change event opens a ~90-day campaign, then closes.** His "a new VP has 90 days to swap vendors" translated: a practice change event — new owner after a sale, a new partner buying in, a new administrator, a relocation just signed — opens a short window when real estate, vendors, and plans are actually up for decision. The radar lanes already CATCH change events (new entity, license move, D12b postings); this adds the TIMING doctrine: outreach lands inside the window, framed to the change ("congratulations on the new practice — the first 90 days are when the lease question is cheapest to ask"), and a caught-signal that sits three months goes stale as a matter of physics, not neglect. The board should age change-event leads accordingly.
2. **Champions are a lane nobody runs: track the PEOPLE from won relationships, not just the practices.** His "champion job changes are your warmest list and they cost nothing," twice over (past-deal champions + lost-deal champions): the office manager, practice administrator, or associate who knew Joe's work on a past deal and then MOVES to a new practice is a warm door into that practice — warmer than any cold signal, and free. The machinery exists as of tonight: they become `party` rows with `works_with`/`knows` edges (intro graph), captured at debrief time like any contact; a debrief question earns its place: "anyone from an old deal turn up somewhere new?"
